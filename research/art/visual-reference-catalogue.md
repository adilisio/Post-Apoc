# Visual Reference Catalogue: Retro-Future, Atomic-Age and Post-Apocalyptic Sources

Purpose: a linked catalogue of the real-world and fictional visual sources behind (and adjacent to) the
post-apocalyptic retro-future look, so the art team can build mood boards for an ORIGINAL game. Nothing here is
to be copied; the catalogue exists to show where the look came from and where it has not been.

Link policy: every link is a stable reference page (Wikipedia, Wikimedia Commons, Library of Congress, archive.org,
museum or artist page). No images were downloaded. Links that could not be checked are tagged `[UNVERIFIED link]`.
Companion file: the sibling per-game art-direction analysis (written separately).

## Quick Reference

- The Fallout look is not one aesthetic. It stacks four: (1) 1939-1964 World's Fair "Raygun Gothic" optimism, (2) 1950s
  Cold War civil-defense and advertising graphics, (3) 1970s-80s grim wasteland cinema (Mad Max 2, A Boy and His Dog), and
  (4) real ruin photography. Each layer has its own source set in sections 1-4.
- Developers on record naming influences: Tim Cain and Leonard Boyarsky (Mad Max 2, A Boy and His Dog, Forbidden Planet,
  Wasteland, X-COM, Cold War posters), Chris Taylor (A Canticle for Leibowitz, Mad Max 2, The City of Lost Children).
  Everything else (Them!, The Day After, On the Beach, La Jetee, EC Comics, Frank R. Paul) is fan or critic inference. See section 3.
- Cain also credited Richard Matheson's I Am Legend, Romero zombie films and 1950s sci-fi films (power armor, robots) per a
  PCGamesN report. The power armor helmet is tied to The City of Lost Children by Cain.
- "Raygun Gothic" (a term coined by William Gibson, 1980s) is the design family: streamlined, chrome, tail-fin, atomic orbit
  motifs, pulp-rocket optimism. The era is roughly 1933-1964.
- The core irony engine: cheerful propaganda aesthetics (Bert the Turtle, Atoms for Peace, Googie diners) overlaid on
  devastation. Section 2 holds the primary material for that tone.
- Nuclear test-site "Doom Town" mannequin houses (Operation Cue 1955, Doorstep 1953) are the real-world seed of the "suburb after
  the bomb" image, and are public-domain US government photography.
- Real decay is more beautiful and more varied than game ruins: Pripyat, Hashima, Centralia, the Salton Sea, Buzludzha. Study
  colour (rust, lichen, peeling paint layers) and light, not just silhouette.
- Rewilding reality: vegetation does NOT politely cover everything evenly. Roofs and gutters fail first, pioneer trees (birch,
  poplar, ailanthus) colonise cracks within 5-20 years, and structural collapse for most buildings is a 50-200 year story (section 4).
- Radiation-driven "giant mutant" biology is mostly implausible. Plausible alternatives: radiation-tolerant fungi, island or
  deep-sea gigantism, parasite-driven behaviour (cordyceps), and abnormal growth forms from disease. See section 5.
- Non-Fallout post-apoc already owns: green reclaimed cities (The Last of Us), Soviet grit and dread (S.T.A.L.K.E.R., Metro),
  fantasy-biology fungus (Nausicaa), desert-chrome (Fury Road), nature-ate-the-robots (Horizon). Do not default to these (section 6).
- Section 8 lists eight under-used aesthetics. Strongest: Japanese Metabolism / Expo '70, Brasilia and Latin American modernism, tropical-modern /
  Afrofuturist independence-era architecture, and 1970s Italian Radical Design.
- Official Fallout art resources (art books, Adam Adamowicz archive) are study-only. Fallout's character comes from a specific
  art-director decision (a 1950s future) and was repeated for 25+ years; the lesson is to make an equally committed decision of our own.
- Caveat: dates, coinages and attributions below are drawn from Wikipedia-level sources plus a few developer-interview reports.
  Where a claim rests on a single or secondary source it is tagged.

## Design Takeaways

- Fallout's identity came from one committed, narrow decision (a 1950s idea of the future). Choose one period or place and commit
  equally hard; a blended mood board produces a generic look.
- The tonal engine is cheerful propaganda against devastation. If we use an optimistic era, source its real ephemera (posters,
  manuals, signage) so the irony is earned, not imitated from Fallout.
- Developer-named sources were few and specific (Mad Max 2, A Boy and His Dog, Forbidden Planet, City of Lost Children, Canticle).
  Mood boards should be small and sharp, with a stated purpose per image, not 500 pins.
- Avoid the Fallout, Last of Us, S.T.A.L.K.E.R./Metro and Fury Road signature palettes (section 6). Use section 8 candidates, and
  prototype a single environment in two candidate aesthetics before committing.
- Real ruins differ from game ruins: they are layered (paint, soot, mineral stain, moss), partial, and uneven. Specify wear by
  material and decade rather than a global dirt overlay.
- Use the rewilding timeline (4b) to date the world explicitly; the chosen "years since" determines the vegetation, structural
  collapse, and what a player can still enter. 10-30 years supports interiors; 100+ years supports ruins as landscape.
- Base creature design on real ecology and morphology (section 5): tolerance, gigantism with a mechanism, parasitism. Keep
  radiation as flavour, not as the explanation, and be consistent about it.
- Mascots and signage (Vault Boy, Bert the Turtle, CONELRAD) show that a simple pictographic language carries a lot of world-building; design our own in-world pictogram system early.
- Study official art books for process (pre-production sketches, material callouts), not for shapes; Adamowicz's Fallout 3 and 4 concept art shows how one artist's palette can define a game.
- Verify cultural aesthetics from section 8 with consultants or natives of those traditions; do not treat them as skins.
