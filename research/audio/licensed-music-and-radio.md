# Fallout Licensed Music and In-Game Radio

Scope: the licensed-oldies device, the intro songs, station tracklists, DJs as reactive narrators, ironic counterpoint, player complaints, licensing economics, and a proposal for an original game. No audio is downloaded or hosted; only links and descriptions.

Method note: web-search budget ran out mid-session. Tracklists below come from fallout.wiki station pages. Years given where the wiki listed them (NV pages only); elsewhere the wiki gave none, so I add a rough "era" only where I am confident and mark the rest as not listed. Where the wiki gives an odd year for a re-recorded version (for example Bill Kenny 1979), that is the version used in-game, not the song's original date.

## Quick Reference

- Core device: cheerful 1930s-50s American pop, swing, jump blues and country played over wreckage. The contrast is the emotional engine, not nostalgia alone.
- Intro songs: FO1 "Maybe" (The Ink Spots, 1940). FO2 "A Kiss to Build a Dream On" (Louis Armstrong). FO3 uses "I Don't Want to Set the World on Fire" (Ink Spots) on Galaxy News Radio.
- FO1 licensing story (verified, fallout.wiki and Wikipedia): the team wanted "I Don't Want to Set the World on Fire", but the rights holder (EMI per fallout.wiki) was slow and asked a high fee, so they chose another Ink Spots song, "Maybe". Tim Cain thought the lyric about being missed suited the ending.
- "Maybe" returned on FO3, FO4 and FO76 radio and in the TV series. In FO76 it is slowed slightly. In FO2 a character, Martin, sings part of it in Vault 8.
- FO3 stations: Galaxy News Radio (20 songs), Enclave Radio (8 patriotic marches and anthems plus 9 speeches), Agatha's violin station (6 pieces), Vault 101 PA (6 big-band tracks).
- NV stations: Radio New Vegas (13 songs plus 6 Gerhard Trede instrumentals), Mojave Music Radio (12 songs plus 3 Bert Weedon instrumentals), Black Mountain Radio (same music, Tabitha's propaganda between tracks).
- FO4: Diamond City Radio (37 songs per fallout.wiki, plus Magnolia's originals), Classical Radio (31 pieces), Radio Freedom (spoken broadcasts).
- FO76: Appalachia Radio lists 50 main-rotation songs on fallout.wiki. APM Music supplied over 100 licensed cues. Brandracket handled supervision.
- "Take Me Home, Country Roads": cover by Spank, E3 2018 teaser; proceeds to Habitat for Humanity, guaranteed at least US$100,000.
- Reactive DJs: Three Dog (Erik Todd Dellums) addresses the player by Karma title. Travis Miles (Brendan Hunt) grows in confidence after a quest. Mr. New Vegas (Wayne Newton) comments on active quests. Julie (Grace Rolek) is an unseen, warm presence in FO76.
- Novelty "atomic" songs are real period records: "Atom Bomb Baby" (The Five Stars), "Uranium Fever" (Elton Britt), "Crawl Out Through the Fallout" (Sheldon Allman), "Uranium Rock" (Warren Smith), "Rocket 69" (Connie Allen).
- FO4 audio director Mark Lampert added the atomic novelty songs after hearing "a pocket of music that [he] hadn't heard before" (Wikipedia paraphrase).
- Licensing is the stated reason for era: William Cheng says the main reason for the 1940s-50s setlists is "reduced licensing costs". Bethesda "fought hard" for Elvis but one song's cost outweighed the variety of the rest (Gibbons per Wikipedia).
- Legal risk is real: Dion DiMucci sued ZeniMax in 2017 over "The Wanderer" in FO4 advertising, seeking over US$1 million; arbitration in 2018.
- Player complaints: tiny playlists and repetition; Polygon's Brian David Gilbert noted only Magnolia and RedEye make new music in a setting running 2102-2287.
- As of Jan 2026 (Cornell chart): US published works 1930 or earlier are public domain; US-published sound recordings before 1926 are public domain. This is the legal basis for a free radio library. Check each recording's status. Not legal advice.

## 1. The device and why it works

| Element | Effect | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-war pop at apocalypse | Dramatic irony: the music is the lost world's voice | Ivanescu calls the intro songs "the first introduction to the world depicted" (Wikipedia) |
| Diegetic radio | The player controls it, tunes it, can turn it off; music has a source | Cheng's Megaton anecdote (Enclave marches during bomb detonation, found "tasteless") |

## 6. Player complaints and mods

- Repetition: with 20 songs (FO3, about 56 minutes) or about 15 (NV), short-form play hits repeats within one session. Diamond City Radio's roughly 37 songs is a third of a day or less.
- Critic complaint: Polygon's Brian David Gilbert noted that only Magnolia and RedEye record new music in a 2102-2287 setting, so the world sounds frozen in 1950 (Wikipedia).
- Stations lose coverage or break: bugs in GNR playback and DJ lines documented on fallout.wiki; Enclave Radio can be lost if Raven Rock is destroyed.
- Mods: radio-expansion mods for FO3, NV and FO4 are widely used (examples include adding custom stations or songs). I did not verify specific mod names, download counts or sites in this pass. [UNVERIFIED] Check Nexus Mods for current standings.
- Official responses: FO4 allowed Magnolia's songs to be added; FO76 expanded to 50 songs plus multiple stations and seasonal additions; Fallout 3 anniversary LP (*Galaxy News Radio*, 2019) pressed licensed music, using 1947 Decca "Easy Living" because the 1937 in-game recording could not be cleared for the record (Wikipedia). This shows that game licence does not equal soundtrack-album licence.

## 8. Proposal: the same emotional effect with a different musical identity

Goal: dramatic irony between a cheerful remembered world and a ruined present, without imitating 1950s Americana and without paying star-catalogue fees.

### 8.1 Choose a different era and genre

| Option | Era/genre | Why it could work | Rights angle |
|---|---|---|---|
| A. Acoustic-recorded era | 1900s-1925 ragtime, brass bands, parlour songs, early jazz, gramophone vaudeville | Sound of cracked shellac is already post-apocalyptic; contrast of jaunty ragtime with ruins | Compositions and many recordings are PD in the US now |
| B. Fictional-nation pastiche | 1960s-70s synth-pop, city pop, cumbia or Euro-lounge written for a fictional country | Lets us design the lost culture from scratch; no real-world baggage | Fully original, owned |
| C. Folk/work-song stratum | Public-domain folk songs sung by hired performers | Communal, diegetic (people singing) rather than radio | Compositions PD, new recordings owned |
| D. Late-century commercial jingles | Invented advertising songs for in-world brands | Absurd-corporate irony (the actual Fallout Vault-Tec/Nuka tone) but ours | Original |
| E. Mixed stations | One station per era (like FO3's 1940s GNR vs Enclave marches vs Bach) | Era becomes faction identity | Combine A, B, D |

### 8.2 Original pastiche songs

- Commission 15-25 songs in the chosen style, written so lyrics double as world-building (state propaganda, product jingles, love songs that read as grief once the world ends).
- Follow the Fallout trick with care: have lyrics that work as innocent in context and ironic after the event, but do not mimic the nuclear-pun jokes of "Atom Bomb Baby"; invent our own catastrophe's vocabulary.
- Include 3-4 "bad" or odd songs on purpose; the FO3 library and stock tracks show that humble pieces carry charm.
- Record with period-appropriate techniques (mono, tape wobble, ribbon mics). A cheap lo-fi chain gives period feel.
- The model of NV's Pete Thomas and Jeff Hooper tracks (2000s recordings in the setlist) and Magnolia's originals (FO4) shows original songs can sit beside licensed ones.

### 8.3 Diegetic radio with a reactive DJ

| Feature | Design |
|---|---|
| Source | Player finds and repairs a transmitter; tuning is a ritual and a minigame |
| Host | A named voice with a flaw that changes (Travis Miles pattern) and a replacement if killed (Three Dog/Margaret pattern) |
| Reactivity | Track a small set of flags (major deeds, faction standing, location discovered) mapped to scripted DJ lines; a few dozen flags, each with 3 variants to avoid repetition |
| Music selection | Weighted shuffle with no-repeat window; time-of-day blocks; songs unlock more after quests |
| Silence | Allow stations to be destroyed, jammed or hijacked (Enclave, Tabitha precedents) |
| Player control | Always allow off/mute; Cheng's "disobedience" anecdote shows players value that |
| Diegetic-physical | Songs play from jukeboxes, wind-up gramophones, street musicians, so music has places |
| Modding | Ship a station-definition format so players can add music (the mods demand shows the need) |

### 8.4 Playlist size targets (my recommendation, derived from the counts above)

| Metric | Fallout | Recommended |
|---|---|---|
| Songs per main station | 12-50 | At least 40 across the main station, plus rotating event sets |
| Spoken-to-music ratio | Not measured | One DJ line every 2-3 songs; 3+ variants for each reaction line |
| Original tracks | 5-10 (Magnolia, RedEye) | 15-25 at launch, more through updates |
| Per-station identity | GNR vs Enclave vs Classical | One strongly distinct palette each |

## Design Takeaways

- The irony device is the product, not the specific era. Choose music that the in-world characters would genuinely love, then let events overturn its meaning.
- Plan for 40+ songs per hub station and a recurring-player model; the FO3 20-song list proved too short.
- Budget original songs from day one. Original pastiche lets lyrics comment on our own world's events; borrowed 1950s hits can only comment on theirs.
- Use public-domain compositions with new recordings as the safe default. Treat famous masters as one-off hero licences and budget for ad-use clearance (Dion).
- Separate game licence from soundtrack/marketing licence in contracts. FO3's vinyl needed a different version of "Easy Living".
- Make the DJ a character with an arc and a failure state; Travis and Three Dog are among the best-loved NPCs because they react.
- Give players a mute and a mod hook for the radio. Their desire to change it is a signal, not a threat.
- Avoid the frozen-in-amber problem: let in-world musicians record new songs as the story advances (Magnolia, RedEye precedents).
