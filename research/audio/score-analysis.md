# Fallout Original Scores: Mark Morgan (FO1/FO2) and Inon Zur (FO3, NV, FO4, FO76)

Scope: the commissioned score, not the licensed radio music (see `licensed-music-and-radio.md`) or sound effects and voice (see `sound-design-and-voice.md`). Research note: the web-search budget ran out mid-session, so some interview material could only be reached second-hand. Gaps are flagged.

## Quick Reference

- Two distinct musical identities exist. FO1/FO2 (Mark Morgan, 1997/1998) are dark ambient, industrial and tribal sound design. FO3/NV/FO4/76 (Inon Zur, 2008-2018) are cinematic orchestral and ambient scores.
- Morgan was a late hire. Per the fallout.wiki biography, another composer was first assigned to FO1 and Charles Deenen brought Morgan in under a tight time constraint. Orchestral music was already covered, so Morgan did the dark ambient material.
- Morgan's method: non-musical sounds taken from sample libraries and his own recordings, processed on a Synclavier (fallout.wiki, Mark Morgan page).
- Morgan has said he works in "Minimalism, Ambient, Tribal, Ethnic and a sound design vibe" (Game Informer, 2014).
- Interplay set specific track lengths and placements, and schedule pressure left no room for rewrites on either game (fallout.wiki).
- FO1 CD: 16 tracks, 56:17, released by Interplay in 1997. FO2 CD: 18 tracks, 58:20, released 1998. Several tracks are shared. Some FO1 material was released free on 10 May 2010 as *Vault Archives* (Wikipedia, Music of the Fallout series).
- Similarity controversy [DISPUTED]: Morgan was accused of resembling Aphex Twin. Wikipedia reports that producer Tim Cain gave him a reference CD of Aphex Twin music with the artist names deliberately omitted. A Medium essay ("Plunderphonics or Plagiarism?") points at the track "City of Lost Angels", but its claim rests on forum lore and the page returned 403, so I could not read it. [UNVERIFIED].
- Brian Eno as a named influence on Morgan's Fallout work: I found no verifiable source. [UNVERIFIED] Treat it as a stylistic comparison only (ambient lineage), not a documented fact.
- FO2 "All-Clear Signal" contains a drum loop sampled from "Busy Child" by The Crystal Method (fallout.wiki). This is a documented sample.
- Zur's FO3 score: American blues and folk blended with military-style music, intended to evoke pre-war American life and "militant progress" (Wikipedia, Fallout 3 article). Critics found it understated: "unintrusive, sparsely textured" (William Cheng).
- NV (2010): Zur wrote tracks 1-35 (2:18:37). Obsidian staff credited on a few others. Zur cited *There Will Be Blood* as an inspiration and worked with audio director Scott Lawlor on string quartet music (Wikipedia, NV article).
- NV reuses Morgan tracks in specific locations and credits him "Additional Music". Examples: "Vats of Goo" is not on the NV list, but "Metallic Monks", "Radiation Storm", "The Vault of the Future", "Underground Troubles", "City of Lost Angels" and "Desert Wind" are (table below).
- FO4 (2015): 65 tracks, 3:37:44. Zur told press the piano is more prominent because the story is more personal, and he blended an acoustic piano with an electric piano for a "round" and "lush" tone. The score has about three times as much music as FO3.
- FO76 (2018): score recorded with the Budapest Film Orchestra and the Lyris String Quartet. Base score 2:52:50, plus *Wastelanders* (1:06:54) and *Steel Dawn* (1:00:12).
- Adaptive principle (NV, documented): exploration music is quiet and ambient and becomes loud and powerful in combat. FO3 uses named special cues (level-up "Success", "Death", "ExitTheVault", location loops).
- Awards: FO3 score nominated for BAFTA and Spike VGA. FO4 nominated for BAFTA Music and The Game Awards Best Score. No Fallout wins listed on Zur's Wikipedia page.
- The one score without licensed 1950s music is *Fallout Tactics* (Zur, 20 ambient tracks), per Wikipedia. That is a useful data point: the licensed songs are what make the franchise sound like Fallout.

| Stylistic stance | "I don't see the point in not sounding like [myself]" | Game Informer 2014 |

### 1.2 Instrumentation and texture (analysis)

Observed from the track descriptions and composer remarks (not from a published instrument list, which I could not find):

- Drones and pads: slow evolving synthesiser beds that stay under dialogue and avoid melodic hooks.
- Industrial percussion: metallic hits, clanks and machine rhythms (track names "Industrial Junk", "Metallic Monks", "Vats of Goo").
- Tribal/ethnic percussion and chant-like voices: used for tribes and cults ("Khans of New California", "Followers' Credo (Thunder)").
- Sparse electric guitar and electronics as accents: FO2 "My Chrysalis Highwayman" adds electric guitar, "All-Clear Signal" adds electronic textures and the sampled drum loop (Wikipedia).
- Result: music that reads as environment, not as a theme. Rémi Lopez describes it as "one moment desolate, the next wild" (Wikipedia citation of Lopez).

| Claim | Evidence | Status |

| Brian Eno influence | No source found | [UNVERIFIED] |
| Morgan's own account | Cites parents' music and being "exposed to a modular synth" as formative (Game Informer) | Verified quotes, but does not name Aphex Twin |

Interpretation: the briefing method (temp tracks or reference CDs from a producer) is a standard industry practice that can blur into imitation. The lesson for us is process: if we give a composer reference music, we must define in writing what may be borrowed (mood, tempo, palette) and what may not (melody, specific sample, signature timbre).

## 2. Inon Zur: biography and approach

- Born in Israel; French horn, piano and composition from childhood; Music Academy of Tel Aviv; moved to the US in 1990; worked in TV animation (Digimon, Power Rangers) before games (fallout.wiki; Wikipedia).
- Says he composes around the emotion wanted at a given moment rather than the setting, and that he needs to play the game first (Wikipedia).
- Fallout credits: *Fallout Tactics* (2001), 3, NV, 4, 76, plus Fallout Shelter material and theme/episode work on the TV series (Wikipedia).
- Direct interview text (IGN, BethBlog, PC Gamer, Level and Gain are listed on his fallout.wiki page) could not be retrieved. I therefore have few verbatim Zur quotes. [GAP]

## 5. Why NV's use of Morgan tracks worked

- Verified: NV plays Morgan pieces in specific locations, and he is credited "Additional Music" (fallout.wiki).
- Verified: Lopez interprets the reuse as homage.
- Analysis, not sourced fan polling: Morgan's drones suit abandoned vaults, ruined towns and research labs, and they signal continuity with the isometric games. Fans rewarded the sense that the franchise remembered itself. Specific fan quotes: [UNVERIFIED], none retrieved.
- Practical benefit: dark-ambient beds are cheap to attach to many interiors and carry no mood cliche of "epic" action.

## Design Takeaways

- Two-tier score strategy: dark ambient/sound-design beds for interiors and desolation, melodic themes saved for hubs and story peaks. Morgan's approach is far cheaper to produce per hour and holds up for 25+ years.
- Make the score instrument-coded by region (as FO4 and Morgan's tribal/industrial split do): players should learn "this timbre equals raiders / machines / safe town".
- Do not ship a temp-track-derived score without a written originality pass. The Aphex Twin dispute shows the reputational cost, even when the briefing method was normal.
- Keep exploration music near-silent with long gaps and let ambience carry; reserve loud cues for fights. NV's documented quiet/loud split is the baseline; add hysteresis so music does not flip every skirmish.
- Build a small set of named event stingers (level-up, death, first-exit, endings). They are cheap and become brand sound.
- Reuse and callback within our own catalogue (as NV did with Morgan) to build continuity across sequels or DLC, but plan this at the licence level for composer credits.
- A hybrid score (ambient bed plus one personal melodic instrument, like FO4 piano) conveys a personal story without dominating.
- Avoid a score so unobtrusive that the licensed songs steal the identity (Kotaku on FO3). Decide up front what our original score's signature sound is.
