# Fallout Development History (1994-2026)

Scope: who made each Fallout game, how long they had, with what engine and money, and which decisions and accidents shaped the result. Purpose: learn production lessons for an ORIGINAL post-apocalyptic game, not to copy Fallout.

Research limits: the search quota ran out partway through, and fandom/IGN/GameSpot-type sites are blocked. Figures below come from Wikipedia, fallout.wiki, and the notes of Tim Cain's GDC 2012 talk. Anything not confirmed by at least one fetched source is marked `[UNVERIFIED]`.

## Quick Reference

- Fallout 1 began in early 1994 as one programmer's spare-time engine (Tim Cain), shipped Oct 1997 after about 3.5 years, for roughly $3M (about $4.5M in 2015 money). Core team about 15 in year two, about 30 in year three; fallout.wiki claims close to 100 credited people at peak [DISPUTED, depends on whether QA/voice/art support is counted].
- It started as a GURPS licence game. Steve Jackson Games withdrew in early 1997 (Interplay: over violence/gore and the "Fallout Boy" art). The team wrote SPECIAL and recoded skills/combat in roughly one to two weeks. Perks came just after.
- Wasteland (1988) was the spiritual ancestor. EA would not license it, so no direct sequel was possible.
- Interplay nearly cancelled Fallout in 1994 and repeatedly afterwards, fearing competition with its own D&D RPGs. Brian Fargo kept it alive; Cain argued for a small budget.
- Fallout 2: announced Dec 1997 as an "11 month" project; Urquhart says financial trouble left "basically nine months". Released Oct 30, 1998. Cain, Boyarsky and Anderson left (Troika founded April 1, 1998). Chris Avellone and other Planescape: Torment designers finished it. Bug-heavy; patch 1.02 in Feb 1999.
- Van Buren (Fallout 3 at Black Isle) was cancelled Dec 8, 2003 as Interplay collapsed. Black Isle was closed in 2004.
- Bethesda bought the Fallout IP from Interplay in April 2007 for $5.75M (earlier, 2004, it had paid a $1.175M minimum advance for the licence). Interplay kept MMO rights on conditions it later failed; Bethesda sued in 2009, settled Jan 2012, Fallout Online died.
- Fallout 3 (Bethesda Game Studios, Gamebryo, 2004-Oct 2008): started with about 10 people while Oblivion shipped, about 75 at the end. Rebuilt from scratch; no Van Buren assets used.
- Fallout: New Vegas (Obsidian, 18 months, Oct 2010) reused the Southwest setting, Legion, Hoover Dam and caravan-war ideas first designed for Van Buren. Missed a Metacritic bonus threshold of 85 by one point.
- Fallout 4: concept art began in 2009, full production mid-2013 to mid-2015, Creation Engine, 111,000 lines of dialogue, 12M copies shipped day one (reported).
- Fallout 76: first explored during FO4, BattleCry (Austin) took the prototype, Creation Engine fought multiplayer, released Nov 14, 2018 to a bad reception; Wastelanders (April 2020) added human NPCs and rescued its reputation.
- Spin-offs show Interplay's decline: Tactics (2001) and Brotherhood of Steel (2004) were outsourced; Shelter (2015) was a hit; the Amazon TV series (2024) re-energised the whole franchise.

## Fallout 1 (Interplay, 1997)

| Item | Detail | Source confidence |
|---|---|---|
| Studio | Interplay Productions (internal team; later "Black Isle" label formed from it) | high |
| Engine | Custom 2D engine by Cain; "cavalier oblique" projection chosen for simpler movement/distance maths and nicer buildings; 640x480, 256 colours, VESA-based | high (GDC notes) |
| Team | Cain alone for 6 months; about 15 in 1995; about 30 in 1996 | high |
| Budget | About $3M | high (Cain, via Game Developer) |
| Key roles | Cain (creator, producer, lead programmer), Christopher Taylor (designer), Leonard Boyarsky (art director, wrote the 1950s retro-future concept), Jason Anderson (lead artist), Mark O'Green (writer), Mark Morgan (music), Feargus Urquhart (producer role, staffing), Brian Fargo (Interplay head; suggested name, requested perks) | high |
| Schedule | About 3.5 years | high |

Important decisions and accidents:

- **Fantasy was rejected** as a saturated market. Other early settings were time travel with dinosaurs and alien invaders. Post-apocalypse was chosen via Wasteland and Road Warrior influences. Boyarsky's pitch was to do the apocalypse "the way movies in the 1950s thought it would happen" (fallout.wiki paraphrase of his quote).
- **Wasteland sequel blocked.** EA refused to licence Wasteland, so Cain's team created a new setting.
- **GURPS loss.** The licence was held from 1994; Interplay dropped it in Feb-Mar 1997 after Steve Jackson Games objected to the tone and violence. Cain says skills and combat were rewritten in about two weeks and that the vision document survived because the team had written down the mood early. SPECIAL's acronym was chosen after Cain's own ACELIPS-style name was rejected.
- **Perks** were requested by Fargo so levelling offered more than skill points; implemented in a day by Taylor.
- **Companions** arrived late, starting from a scripter's dog idea; some bugs (an NPC shooting the player) could not be fixed because game scripts had no AI access.
- **Cancellation risk.** Interplay acquired D&D licences in 1994 and almost killed Fallout; Cain says internal staff tried several times.
- **Rating and regional edits.** Aimed for Teen, received Mature. Killing children was possible; the European release removed children (v1.2).
- **Platform quirk.** The game failed Windows 95 certification because it also ran on NT; the team made the installer refuse NT while allowing manual installs.
- **Music.** The Ink Spots were chosen for the 1940s-50s feel; first-choice song licence was too slow, "Maybe" used instead.
- **Cut time-travel concept.** An early draft had a time-travelling hero who causes the ape ancestor's death (Destructoid/GDC).

## Fallout 2 (Black Isle Studios / Interplay, 1998)

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| Studio | Black Isle Studios (Interplay division led by Urquhart) |
| Engine | Upgraded Fallout 1 engine; marketing wanted a 16-bit upgrade but that would have meant redoing all art, so it was not done |
| Schedule | Full development began Oct 1997; announced Feb 13, 1998; gold Oct 22; released Oct 30, 1998 |
| Team | Most of the original team initially; Urquhart and Matt Norton lead designers; Gary Platner art director; Eric DeMilt co-producer; four designers brought from Planescape: Torment including Chris Avellone |
| Budget | [UNVERIFIED] |

Decisions and accidents:

- Cain announced "11 months"; Urquhart later said financial trouble gave "basically nine months to make the whole game". Staff were moved from Planescape: Torment and the team crunched.
- Cain left a few months into development. Reasons given on Wikipedia and fallout.wiki: burnout, a bonus cut after refusing to name who caused a crash bug, and friction over marketing influence and rejected box art. He said the team was "losing part of the game to a larger group who had bigger plans for it." He also said the team never intended to build a franchise: "We didn't sign up to make a franchise."
- Boyarsky says he, Cain and Anderson wrote the main story arc, redesigned followers and designed many quests (Vault City among them) before they left. Boyarsky, Cain and Anderson founded **Troika Games** on April 1, 1998 (name: "three of a kind"). Troika made Arcanum (2001), Temple of Elemental Evil (2003), Vampire: Bloodlines (2004) and closed Feb 24, 2005 after failing to fund a post-apocalyptic RPG. Cain later expressed disappointment that Troika could not buy Fallout from Interplay.
- Remaining designers were handed unfinished designs. Avellone said this gave an uneven experience and elsewhere called the game "a slapdash project without a lot of oversight."
- Doubling the world size on a short schedule created many bugs (e.g. the drivable car's trunk detaching, fixed only in patch 1.02d, nearly four months after launch). Interplay's last official patch, 1.02, came Feb 11, 1999.
- Reception: Metacritic 86; sold well in the US, weaker in the UK (Eurogamer report via Wikipedia).
- Weapon mod systems (laser sights, grenade launchers) were trimmed to a limited upgrade system; a full-auto mode and expansion were considered.

## Interplay's Decline, Van Buren, and the Sale

- **Van Buren** (Black Isle's Fallout 3): a false start after Fallout 2, then a 2002-2003 effort. Chris Avellone wrote the vision document and led it for about three years; Josh Sawyer became lead designer, with Damien Foletto, John Deiley, Sean K. Reynolds; Cain consulted on the schedule. Engine: the in-house "Jefferson" engine; NDL (later Gamebryo) was considered. The publisher (per Chad Nicholas on fallout.wiki) mandated real-time combat and multiplayer, which led to the dual-mode design. Sawyer left Black Isle in Nov 2003 saying "I lost faith". Cancelled Dec 8, 2003 in favour of Fallout: Brotherhood of Steel; Black Isle closed two weeks after Sawyer's departure.
- **Leaks:** design documents (Wikipedia says a 700-page one) leaked in 2005-2006 and a playable tutorial technical demo appeared in 2007.
- **Bethesda deals:** 2004 licence for Fallout 3 (minimum guaranteed advance $1.175M against royalties); April 2007 purchase of the IP for $5.75M, with Interplay licensed to make an MMO if it hit conditions (start within 24 months, raise $30M, ship within four years, 12% revenue share).
- **Fallout Online / Project V13:** Interplay hired Jason Anderson as creative director (Nov 2007-Mar 2009) and signed with Masthead Studios (Bulgaria) in April 2009. Bethesda moved to rescind (Apr 2009), sued (Sept 2009), lost a preliminary injunction request (Dec 2009), and settled in Jan 2012 [DISPUTED: Wikipedia says Jan 2; fallout.wiki says Jan 10], returning all rights to Bethesda. Reported payment to Interplay was about $2M [UNVERIFIED in this research]. A later "Black Isle" relaunch attempt (Dec 2012) raised just over $6,000 and went nowhere.

## Fallout 3 (Bethesda Game Studios, 2008)

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| Studio | Bethesda Game Studios (Rockville, MD) |
| Engine | Gamebryo (same family as Oblivion) |
| Team | About 10 at start in late 2004 (most staff on Oblivion); about 75 by the end (programmer Jean Simonet) |
| Key people | Todd Howard (executive producer/director), Emil Pagliarulo (lead designer and writer), Istvan Pely (lead artist), Joel Burgess (lead level designer), Steve Meister (lead programmer), producers Ashley Cheng, Gavin Carter, Jeff Gardiner, Pete Hines (PR) |
| Budget | [UNVERIFIED] |

Key facts:

- Rebuilt from scratch; no Van Buren code or assets reused, though the Fallout Bible (compiled by Avellone, 2002) was used as inspiration.
- Howard studied the first game's manual and hint book; he preferred its humour to Fallout 2's pop-culture jokes and fourth-wall breaks, and became more interested in the pre-war world.
- **Design shift:** from the old isometric party/turn-based game to a first-person open-world RPG ("Oblivion with guns" was the early jibe). V.A.T.S. was invented so first-person play would still feel like a stats-driven RPG; Howard described it as "Burnout crash mode with body parts" (fallout.wiki). Pagliarulo wanted immersive-sim elements (Deus Ex) and better stealth.
- **Setting moved** from the West Coast to Washington D.C. early on. The engine forced D.C. to be chopped into zones linked by the Metro; playtesters disliked it. Joel Burgess later called D.C. "the big mistake" [as paraphrased by Wikipedia; exact wording not verified].
- Cuts: downtown D.C. was originally about twice as big (26 enclosed areas vs 12); about 30% of dialogue was cut late to fit voiced lines on the disc (Fred Zeleny); five skills removed; two months lost to enlarging the north and west map in alpha.
- Won Game Developers Choice 2008 Game of the Year and Best Writing.

## Fallout: New Vegas (Obsidian, 2010)

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| Studio | Obsidian Entertainment (founded by Urquhart, Avellone and others; many ex-Black Isle staff) |
| Publisher | Bethesda Softworks |
| Engine | Gamebryo from Fallout 3; Obsidian had no experience, hired a modder, struggled with Havok and console memory (PS3 split memory forced disabled effects and removal of a casino entryway) |
| Schedule | 18 months, with "heavy overtime and mandatory crunch weekends" per Sawyer on fallout.wiki (Wikipedia says 40-50 hour weeks) [DISPUTED] |
| Team size | [UNVERIFIED] |
| Key people | Josh Sawyer (project director/lead designer), John Gonzalez (lead creative designer), Chris Avellone (senior designer; Ulysses, Cassidy), Joe Sanabria (lead art) |
| Sales | Bethesda said 5M copies and about $300M revenue by Nov 2010 (GamesRadar via Wikipedia) |

Key facts:

- Obsidian pitched a three-page document; working titles Sin City, then Vegas, then New Vegas. Source code access was delayed by Bethesda's security clearance, so the first months used only the tools.
- Howard lobbied for a full game instead of an expansion. Story overview went through five iterations (Gonzalez).
- Sawyer wanted themes of greed and excess drawn from Las Vegas, "recreating the new world in the image of the old", Western imagery; he scouted the Mojave on a motorcycle.
- **Van Buren reuse:** Southwest setting, Hoover Dam, Caesar's Legion, caravan wars, NCR-versus-others tensions came from Van Buren documents (Avellone wrote or inspired much of it, Sawyer carried it into New Vegas).
- Bethesda's bonus: 85+ Metacritic on any platform; missed by one point. Urquhart on the contract: "It was in the contract, it was what it said."
- Launch was very buggy; patches followed in Nov-Dec 2010. Six DLC were led by Avellone and Sawyer (Dead Money, Honest Hearts, Old World Blues, Lonesome Road, plus two small packs).

## Fallout 4 (Bethesda Game Studios, 2015)

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| Studio | Bethesda Game Studios (Rockville, with help from sister studios) |
| Engine | Creation Engine (from Skyrim, heavily modified) with a new face editor and PBR-style lighting [PBR claim UNVERIFIED] |
| Timeline | Concept art started a week after the final Fallout 3 DLC (2009); Skyrim took priority until 2011-2013; full production mid-2013 to mid-2015; reveal June 2015; release Nov 10, 2015 |
| Team / budget | [UNVERIFIED] |
| Key people | Todd Howard (director), Emil Pagliarulo (lead designer/writer, also voices Parker Quinn), Istvan Pely (art), Jeff Gardiner (producer) |

Key facts:

- Long pre-production: Howard's early idea was to explore the world before the bombs fell (hence the opening day of normal life). The team visited Boston for photos. The T-60 power armour went through seven years of iterations. Lead concept artist Adam Adamowicz died in 2012 but his work shaped the game.
- Voiced protagonist (male and female actors, 111,000 lines of dialogue, more than FO3 and Skyrim combined). Code name "Angelina" kept the voice sessions secret.
- Producer Jeff Gardiner said the workshop/settlement feature was the likeliest thing to be cut for schedule reasons, but the team kept it.
- Howard cited GTA V as the model for "complete freedom".
- 12M copies shipped to retail/digital in one day (reported), over $750M in 24 hours (Fortune, via Wikipedia).
- Kotaku leaked casting documents in Dec 2013 that turned out to be Fallout 4.

## Fallout 76 (Bethesda Game Studios, 2018)

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| Studio | Bethesda Game Studios Rockville (lead) with the Austin studio formed from BattleCry Studios, plus help from Arkane, id, ZeniMax Online, Iron Galaxy, Double Eleven (credits in Wikipedia notes) |
| Engine | Creation Engine, built for single-player; relied on legacy code; BattleCry proposed using Quake-era netcode ideas |
| Origins | Multiplayer concepts shelved during FO4 as too large; after FO4 shipped (Nov 2015) a Fallout multiplayer project was greenlit and BattleCry (Austin), which had paused its own free-to-play game, took it on |
| Release | Announced May 30, 2018; E3 June 10; released Nov 14, 2018 |
| Team size | [UNVERIFIED]. Kotaku (Schreier-style reporting, via Wikipedia) cited weak leadership, high turnover, heavy crunch, and bugs QA flagged but could not get fixed fast; staff from Starfield and Redfall were moved to help |

Key facts:

- Rural Appalachia chosen to provide a bigger map; human NPCs were initially omitted (lore: 25 years after bombs; gameplay: players are the humans), replaced by robots, holotapes and terminals. Players found this empty.
- Launch problems: bugs, no beta; Howard later said he regretted not running a beta and argued "It's not how you launch, it's what it becomes."
- Canvas bag fiasco: Power Armor edition shipped nylon; compensation of 500 Atoms; canvas bags delivered June 2019. A support glitch exposed data of about 65 customers (Dec 2018).
- Post-launch: Wastelanders (April 2020, human NPCs and factions) was the turnaround; Fallout 1st subscription ($12.99/month, Oct 2019) drew criticism; modes added and removed: Survival (beta Mar 26, 2019, removed Oct 1, 2019), Nuclear Winter battle royale (June 10, 2019, removed late 2021), Vault Raids (removed Apr 14, 2020). Player count peaked in 2024 after the TV show. Native PS5/Xbox Series versions: Sept 15, 2026 per Wikipedia [single source].

## Cross-Cutting Observations

- Three generations of owners: Interplay (a small, talented, underfunded team), Black Isle/Obsidian (reactive design culture, short schedules), Bethesda (long production, systemic open world, moddable engine).
- Short schedules recur: FO1 (SPECIAL and core rules rewritten in about two weeks), FO2 (about nine months), NV (18 months). Each produced cuts and bugs but also concentrated design.
- Engine inheritance shaped design: custom 2D engine, Gamebryo with chopped cities, Creation Engine ill-suited for MMO.

## Design Takeaways

- **Write the vision down before the licence or engine are stable.** Fallout survived losing GURPS because its mood and rules of tone were already documented; build the pitch document first and keep it short.
- **Protect an early prototype from the publisher's other priorities.** Fallout nearly died twice because it competed with in-house franchises; ship a cheap, legible vertical slice.
- **Do not rush a sequel to the same engine on a third of the time.** FO2 and NV both show that scope grows while schedule shrinks; fix scope first, content later.
- **Pick the setting your engine can render.** FO3's chopped D.C. and 76's single-player engine turned into design problems for years. Choose geography around technical limits.
- **Beta-test multiplayer or live-service launches publicly.** Howard's own regret about no beta for 76 is the clearest lesson.
- **Do not strip the world of people to simplify an MMO.** 76 without human NPCs felt hollow and needed a major patch to recover.
- **Keep teams and design leadership stable.** The Cain/Boyarsky/Anderson exit left FO2 uneven; Troika then ran out of funding. Hand-offs mid-development cost cohesion.
- **Tie bonuses to quality carefully.** The one-point Metacritic miss illustrates how a publisher incentive can sour a good collaboration.
- **Treat licensing and IP ownership as a design risk.** The licence chain (GURPS, Wasteland, Interplay, Bethesda) shaped every game; own your IP and its tone from day one.
