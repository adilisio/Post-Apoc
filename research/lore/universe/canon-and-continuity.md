# Fallout Universe — Canon and Continuity

What counts, who decides, where the seams show, and what that teaches us about keeping a setting bible.

Tag key: `[DISPUTED]` · `[RETCON]` · `[SEMI-CANON]` · `[TV]` (Amazon series) · `[UNVERIFIED]` = recalled from
general knowledge and not re-checked against a source in this session (Nukapedia blocked automated access).

## Quick Reference

- **The IP holder decides.** Since buying the IP in 2007, Bethesda defines canon; it has never published a
  formal tiered policy, only scattered statements.
- **Core canon:** Fallout, Fallout 2, Fallout 3, New Vegas, Fallout 4, Fallout 76, and (per Todd Howard,
  2024) the TV series.
- **Fallout Tactics:** broad events acknowledged, details not — `[SEMI-CANON]`.
- **Fallout: Brotherhood of Steel (console, 2004):** non-canon.
- Todd Howard, 2007: "For our purposes, neither Fallout Tactics nor Fallout: Brotherhood of Steel happened."
  Fallout 3 nonetheless mentions a Brotherhood detachment around Chicago.
- **Van Buren** (Black Isle's cancelled Fallout 3): never released, non-canon, but heavily mined for New Vegas.
- **Fallout Bible** (Avellone, 2002): not canon by its own author's account, yet it supplies most pre-2242
  dates that later games adopted.
- **TV series:** canon, set in 2296, the latest point on the timeline; produced with Howard as executive
  producer.
- Howard on the show: "Everything that happened in the previous games, including New Vegas, happened."
- **No New Vegas ending is canon**; the show sets itself ~15 years later and stays vague.
- The biggest disputes are (1) Interplay-era tone and facts vs Bethesda's reinterpretation, (2) Fallout 76
  as a prequel, and (3) the show's destruction of Shady Sands and the NCR.
- The two major fan wikis handle this differently; fallout.wiki explicitly refuses to rule on canon and
  treats all licensed sources as equally valid records.
- Bethesda's habit: **keep everything, reinterpret freely, explain later (or not)**. Interplay's habit:
  **improvise, then rationalise in the Bible**.
- Most "retcons" began as an art decision or a joke that later writers had to justify.

### The Shady Sands controversy

1. **April 2024.** Season 1 shows a classroom chalkboard: "2277 — The Fall of Shady Sands" with an arrow to
   a drawn mushroom cloud. Viewers read this as "nuked in 2277", which would erase New Vegas (2281), in
   which the NCR is at full strength.
2. **Pagliarulo (design director, Bethesda)** replied publicly that New Vegas is canon and posted a
   timeline: Fallout 3 2277, New Vegas 2281, Fallout 4 2287, show 2296.
3. **Howard (IGN interview)** said the bomb "falls just after the events of New Vegas", and that "the Fall"
   refers to a decline that began earlier. He acknowledged they were threading it tightly.
4. **Season 2 (Dec 2025)** dramatises the bombing in a flashback; one secondary source dates it 2283
   `[UNVERIFIED]`.
5. Residual objections `[DISPUTED]`: the city's apparent relocation toward Los Angeles; whether one bomb
   plausibly collapses a multi-state republic; and the sense among Black Isle/Obsidian fans that a
   West-Coast achievement was cleared away for a blanker canvas.

Context: Chris Avellone has said he wanted New Vegas's *Lonesome Road* to give players the option of
nuking the NCR for much the same reason — too much civilisation undermines the genre `[UNVERIFIED]`. The
impulse to reset is not unique to Bethesda.

### Tim Cain's later statements

Cain's YouTube channel (2023 onward) has become a parallel source of "original intent": China fired first
over FEV; the Vaults were starship research; there was no single divergence point. He is reported as
stressing these are what the 1997 team believed, mostly unwritten, and that the current owner defines
canon. Effect: fans now cite **three** layers — in-game text, the Bible, and creator recollection — and
they do not always agree.

## 3. How each studio treated continuity

| Era | Practice | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Interplay, FO1 (1997) | Minimal backstory; manual plus intro; mysteries left alone | Evocative; nothing to contradict | Almost nothing written down — later teams had to guess |
| Black Isle, FO2 (1998) | Rapid development by many designers; bold additions (Vault experiment, Enclave); loose tone control | Expanded the world enormously | Inconsistent tone, anachronistic jokes |
| Avellone's Fallout Bible (2002) | Retroactive codification, answering fan questions in public, openly correcting itself | First unified timeline; honest about uncertainty | One person's reading; published corrections created a second layer of disputes; never finished |
| Micro Forté / Interplay spin-offs (2001–04) | Little coordination with core lore | — | Led directly to both being disowned |
| Bethesda, FO3–4 (2008–15) | Treat earlier mainline games as true; move to a far region; reuse iconic elements with new local explanations | Avoids contradicting the West; recognisable brand | "Greatest hits" repetition; additive retcons; pre-war lore inflation |
| Obsidian, NV (2010) | Direct sequel to the West-Coast story by original-era staff; advanced the timeline's politics | Most internally consistent entry; societies actually change | Created a state of the world later owners found constraining |
| Bethesda, FO76 (2018) | Prequel only 25 years after the war, with familiar factions inserted | Fresh blank region | Requires elaborate justifications; most retcon complaints |
| Kilter/Bethesda, TV (2024–) | Declared canon; set last on the timeline; changes the world rather than adapting a game | Moves the story forward; avoids player-choice conflicts | A single prop triggered a canon crisis; resolves a rival studio's storyline off-screen |

## 4. Lessons for maintaining a setting bible

| Fallout problem | Root cause | Practice for us |
|---|---|---|
| Vault purpose, ghoul origin, first strike all "decided" years later by interview | Intent lived in people's heads | Write intent down at the time, including "this is deliberately unanswered" |
| Bible dates vs game dates vs guide dates | Multiple unsynchronised authorities | One versioned timeline file; everything else references it |
| Chalkboard incident | Set dressing not checked against the timeline | Lore review for every on-screen date, map and document |
| Tactics/BoS disowned | Spin-offs without lore oversight | Define canon tiers before licensing or outsourcing anything |
| Super mutants and Brotherhood everywhere | Brand recognition overriding regional logic | Decide which elements are universal and which are regional *by rule* |
| 76's contortions | Prequel in an established timeline | Prefer new places over earlier times; reserve empty decades on purpose |
| Shady Sands | Sequel needs wasteland, canon has a nation | Decide the long-term recovery curve of the world up front |
| Unreliable but treated as fact | In-world documents quoted as truth | Tag every lore entry "narrator truth" or "in-world claim (by whom)" |

## Gaps

- No official Bethesda canon policy document exists to cite; the status table is assembled from individual
  statements, several known only through secondary reporting.
- Avellone's and Sawyer's own remarks on the Bible and on canon were not read in primary form this session.
- The Vault (fallout-archive) and Nukapedia canon pages were blocked (HTTP 402); the fallout.wiki policy
  page was read instead.
- Status of Fallout Shelter and the tabletop games is unconfirmed.

## Design Takeaways

- **Publish (internally) a canon policy on day one**: tiers (core game text > official supplementary >
  developer commentary > licensed spin-offs), and who can change them. Fallout still lacks this after 29 years.
- **Keep a "sealed mysteries" list.** Questions that must never be answered, with the reason. The first
  strike is Fallout's best example; it survived only by luck and by Cain's caveats.
- **Separate truth from testimony in the bible itself.** Most of Fallout's flexibility comes from lore
  delivered by biased characters. Record what is objectively true (for writers) apart from what characters
  believe (for players).
- **Record aesthetic choices with their justification, or mark them as unexplained on purpose.** The
  13-star flag was chosen for looks and spawned a whole political structure. That can be a gift or a trap.
- **Plan for the reset temptation.** If the fiction lets civilisation rebuild, later instalments will want
  to knock it down. Either build a setting whose pressures keep it unstable, or commit to showing
  reconstruction as the story. Do not undo it between instalments.
- **Never erase another team's ending off-screen.** If a status quo has to change, make the change a
  playable or watchable event in its own right, with causes the audience can see.
- **Additive beats subtractive.** Fans tolerated new FEV strains and new Brotherhood chapters far better
  than they tolerate anything being removed or contradicted.
- **Do not let creator interviews become a shadow canon.** Agree what the team will and will not say
  publicly about unanswered questions.
- **Treat spin-offs as real or do not make them.** Disowning two shipped games cost goodwill and left
  permanent "semi-canon" clutter.
- **Give regions their own ecology of factions and monsters.** Reusing the mascots everywhere is the
  cheapest route to recognisability and the most damaging to coherence.
- **Budget for a lore keeper.** The Bible worked because one person answered questions consistently, and
  failed where it was one person's unpaid side project.
