# Vaults and the Vault-Tec Experiment Program

Scope: Fallout 1, 2, 3, New Vegas, 4 and 76, with spin-offs and the TV series noted only where they change
the picture. This file describes and analyses; it does not reproduce game text.

## Quick Reference

- A vault is a sealed underground fallout shelter built by the Vault-Tec Corporation under a US government
  programme (named "Project Safehouse" in later lore). Publicly: salvation. Secretly: almost every vault ran
  an experiment on its residents.
- **The experiment idea did not exist in Fallout 1 (1997).** There, vaults are just shelters; Vault 12's
  broken door and Vault 13's failing water chip read as misfortune. `[RETCON]`
- The "vaults were social experiments" twist was introduced in **Fallout 2 (1998)** through the Enclave's
  leadership, and credited to Tim Cain (with Leonard Boyarsky and Jason Anderson) during early Fallout 2
  development before they left Interplay.
- Chris Avellone's **Fallout Bible (2002)** codified it: 122 vaults commissioned, a list of ~15 example
  experiments, and the admission that he did not know Cain's original reasoning.
- In 2023 Tim Cain said the original reason was that the Enclave wanted data for a **multi-generation
  starship**; each vault tested a system or a social condition the ship would face. This is the creator's
  intent, never stated in a game. `[SEMI-CANON]`
- Bethesda-era games treat the experiments as increasingly sadistic or absurd behavioural science, with the
  observers long dead. The purpose is rarely stated and is not needed for the stories to work.
- The Fallout: The Roleplaying Game rulebook (Modiphius) gives **17 control vaults and 105 experimental
  vaults**. The "17 controls" figure originates in a Fallout 3 terminal. `[SEMI-CANON]`
- Named control vaults: 3, 8, 13 (disputed), 76. Vault 81 becomes a de facto control because its first
  overseer sabotaged the experiment.
- Roughly 45 numbered vaults are documented across games, comics and the show; about 30 are physically
  visitable in the six main games.
- **Bethesda's 2008 Penny Arcade comic** ("One Man, and a Crate of Puppets") pushed the idea towards black
  comedy (Vault 77: one man and puppets; Vault 69: one man, 999 women). It is official promo material whose
  canon status rests on an in-game jumpsuit and holotape. `[SEMI-CANON]`
- Vaults function as the series' **anthology slot**: each is a closed-room short story with a one-line
  premise, a cast that is already dead, and an ending the player reconstructs backwards.
- Most consistently praised: **Vault 11** (New Vegas) by a wide margin; then 22, 34, 112, 108, 106, 92, 87,
  95, 81, 75, 118, 94 and 51.
- The format's weak point is escalation: once every vault is a death trap, the question "why would anyone
  build these?" gets louder, and the corporation becomes a cartoon.
- The vault jumpsuit, gear-shaped door and blue-and-yellow branding are the single most recognisable visual
  asset the series owns.

## 1. What a vault is

| Aspect | Detail |
|---|---|
| Builder | Vault-Tec Corporation (subsidiary/partner relationships with the US government; later lore adds RobCo and other contractors as suppliers) |
| Programme name | Project Safehouse (name used in Bethesda-era lore); financed with public money and "junk bonds" per the Fallout Bible |
| Commissioned | 122 vaults in the Fallout Bible and RPG rulebook; a pre-war newscaster line says "well over 100". Real total "unknowable" per the RPG book |
| Nominal capacity | ~1,000 residents each (Vault 13's design figure in Fallout 1); Vault 76 held ~500 |
| Standard kit | Fusion/geothermal power, water purification chip, food synthesis, hydroponics, armoury, overseer's office, jumpsuits, Pip-Boys |
| Intended seal time | Varies by experiment: 6 months to "forever". Control vaults 10-25 years |
| Leadership | A single Overseer with sealed instructions; residents usually do not know the experiment |
| The G.E.C.K. | A "Garden of Eden Creation Kit" terraforming package issued to selected vaults for the day the door opens (see `technology-and-items.md`) |
| Door | Gear-shaped rolling blast door (Fallout 1-3, NV, 76) or vertical/lift entry (111 in Fallout 4) |

The arithmetic is itself a story beat: 122 shelters of 1,000 people for a nation of hundreds of millions
means the programme was never capable of saving the population. The Fallout Bible spells this out; the games
mostly leave players to notice.

## 2. Origin of the "experiment" idea

| Date | Event | Significance |
|---|---|---|
| 1997 | Fallout 1 ships | Vaults are shelters. Vault 12 failing, Vault 15 collapsing and Vault 13's chip breaking are presented as ordinary failure. No conspiracy. |
| 1997-98 | Early Fallout 2 design | Per fallout.wiki's behind-the-scenes notes, Cain, Boyarsky and Anderson conceive of vaults "housing dark experiments". All three leave Interplay in early 1998. |
| 1998 | Fallout 2 ships | The Enclave's President reveals the vaults were a deliberate social experiment and that the Enclave (the surviving US government/corporate elite on an oil rig) is the heir to the observers. Vault 13's long closure and Vault 8's control status are explained. `[RETCON]` of Fallout 1. |
| 2002 | Fallout Bible 0-9 (Avellone) | Lists experiments for Vaults 8, 12, 13, 15, 27, 29, 34, 36, 42, 53, 55, 56, 68, 69, 70, 106. Credits the idea to Cain; admits the rationale is partly speculative; leaves the rest "open for fan fiction". |
| 2003 | Van Buren (cancelled) | Would have used Bible vaults (e.g. 29, 70). `[SEMI-CANON]` |
| 2008 | Fallout 3 | Bethesda adopts the Bible list wholesale (a Citadel terminal lists local vaults; Vault 106 is taken straight from the Bible) and makes the experiment-dungeon a core content type. |
| 2008 | Penny Arcade comic, 12 strips on the official site | Vault 77 (one man, a crate of puppets), plus one-liners for 43 (20 men, 10 women, one panther) and 69. Sets the black-comedy register. |
| 2010 | New Vegas | Obsidian (with Avellone and ex-Black Isle staff) builds Vault 34 from its Bible entry and writes Vault 11, the format's high point. |
| 2015-18 | Fallout 4, Vault-Tec Workshop DLC | Vault 88 lets the player *be* the experimenting overseer. |
| 2018-24 | Fallout 76 | Vaults 51, 63, 79, 94, 96 used as raids, a battle-royale mode and expansion hubs. |
| 2023 | Tim Cain YouTube video | States the original intent: the Enclave planned a generation starship and used vaults as test beds; "every vault was in some sense a test", including controls. |
| 2024- | TV series | Adds Vaults 4, 24, 31, 32, 33 and a boardroom scene in which corporations bid for experiments and Vault-Tec management contemplates starting the war itself. `[DISPUTED]` among fans as a shift of blame from governments to one company. |

### The competing rationales

| Rationale | Source | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Data for a generation starship (isolation, overcrowding, equipment failure, leadership) | Tim Cain, 2023 | Explains "boring" Bible experiments such as failing equipment (53) or missing entertainment (55/56). Never in a game. |
| Study of how selected populations cope with isolation and whether they can recolonise | Fallout Bible 0 | Avellone's best reconstruction; flagged by him as speculative. |
| The Enclave as observer, planning to repopulate with "pure" humans | Fallout 2 | The only fully in-game Black Isle explanation. |
| Vault-Tec corporate R&D / sadism with no surviving observer | Fallout 3, 4, 76 (implicit) | Experiments become individually authored horror or comedy. Vault 88 and Vault-Tec University show prototype-testing culture. |
| Management wants to outlive competitors and "win" capitalism | TV series | Latest layer; `[RETCON]` relative to the Enclave framing. |

Note that Fallout Bible material is no longer treated as binding canon (Avellone has said as much), although
Bethesda and Obsidian both mined it.

### Control vaults

| Vault | Why it counts | Result |
|---|---|---|
| 8 | Bible/Fallout 2: control, open after 10 years, issued a G.E.C.K. | Became Vault City: prosperous, xenophobic, slave-holding in all but name. The control itself produces a morally grey society. |
| 13 | Fallout 1: an ordinary vault. Bible: meant to stay shut 200 years as an isolation study. fallout.wiki calls it a control "on standby". `[DISPUTED]` | Produced the Vault Dweller; later raided by the Enclave. |
| 3 | New Vegas: no experiment | Opened voluntarily, traded, and was massacred by the Fiends. A control killed by the outside world. |
| 76 | Fallout 3 terminal: control, 500 residents, open after 20 years. Fallout 76: opens "Reclamation Day", 23 Oct 2102 (25 years after the war) `[RETCON]` of the 20-year figure | Player characters; an MMO lobby explained in fiction. |
| 81 | Not a control by design; first overseer cut the science staff off | Still inhabited in 2287; the "what if a vault just worked" settlement. |

Design lesson already visible: control vaults are interesting *because* the baseline around them is horror.

## 4. Why vaults work as short stories

### 4.1 The structural recipe

1. **A one-sentence premise.** Every memorable vault compresses to a pitch you can repeat to a friend:
   "everyone but one man is a puppet", "they must vote someone to death every year", "a vault for recovering
   addicts, with a hidden stash". The rule is the hook.
2. **A sealed room.** The door isolates variables. No outside faction, no trade, no rescue. Whatever happened
   was caused by the premise plus human nature, which is why the stories feel like parables.
3. **The player arrives after the ending.** The cast is dead or transformed. The story is told as
   archaeology: terminals, audio logs, skeleton tableaux, propaganda posters, a locked door with claw marks.
4. **Reverse chronology by geography.** The entrance shows the aftermath; the deepest room (overseer's
   office, reactor, lab) holds the cause. Physical depth equals narrative depth, so pacing takes care of
   itself: the player cannot reach the explanation without walking through its consequences.
5. **Dramatic irony from the brand.** Cheerful Vault-Tec signage, the mascot, and intercom jingles play
   against what is on the floor. The same asset kit is reassuring in the tutorial vault and sinister
   everywhere else.
6. **A final reveal or a final choice.** The best vaults end with a turn (Vault 11's message, Vault 112's
   failsafe) or a decision with a cost (Vault 34: save trapped survivors or stop the radiation poisoning the
   farms above; Vault 81: who gets the single dose of cure).
7. **A reusable tile set.** For the developer, a vault is the cheapest dungeon to build: one modular
   corridor kit, redressed with one bespoke gimmick (plants, water, gas, clones). The anthology format is
   also a production strategy.

### 4.4 Where the format fails

- **Escalation into cartoon evil.** The Bible's experiments are mundane (dim bulbs, bad food) and plausible
  as data-gathering. Later vaults are atrocities with no conceivable observer. Each is a good story;
  together they make the institution unbelievable.
- **No observer, no point.** Few vaults show anyone collecting results. When the "why" is absent, the
  premise is carrying the whole experience.
- **Joke vaults date quickly.** 69 and 77 work as one-panel gags, less well as places.
- **Premise without payoff.** Vault 75 and Vault 114 (Fallout 4) have strong pitches delivered mainly through
  a couple of terminals in a combat corridor. Fallout 4's vaults are often cited as weaker *levels* than
  Fallout 3's or New Vegas's even when the ideas are as good.
- **Tile-set fatigue.** Identical grey corridors make navigation frustrating (Fallout 3's vaults and New
  Vegas's Vault 34 are frequent complaints) unless a gimmick breaks up the space.
- **The retcon tax.** Fallout 1's vaults were not written as experiments, so 12, 13 and 15 needed
  after-the-fact rationales, and 13's status is still argued over.

No authoritative ranking exists; outlets publish lists (PC Gamer, GamesRadar, Gameranx, TheGamer, Twinfinite,
Prima, WatchMojo) with different criteria ("best story", "creepiest", "most survivable"). I could not
retrieve the full text of the PC Gamer and GamesRadar rankings, so the ordering below is a synthesis of
recurring mentions and long-standing community consensus, not a transcription. `[UNVERIFIED ordering]`

| Tier | Vaults | Recurring reason given |
|---|---|---|
| Consensus best | 11 (NV) | Best-written twist; storytelling almost entirely environmental; often called the best location in the series |
| Near-universal top five | 112, 108 (3); 22, 34 (NV) | Unique mechanics or atmosphere; memorable one-line hook |
| Frequently listed | 87, 92, 106, 101 (3); 95, 81, 111, 118 (4); 21, 19 (NV) | Strong premise; main-quest importance (87, 101, 111) |
| Best of 76 | 51, 94, 63 | Story strength relative to an online game; 63 praised as a return to single-player-style quest writing |

what they worked out, not what they shot.

## Design Takeaways

- **Build an anthology container into the world.** A repeatable, sealed, self-explanatory location type
  (ours must be original: not numbered corporate shelters) gives writers a slot for standalone short stories
  and gives level designers a reusable kit. Decide the container first, then the stories.
- **One rule per site, statable in a sentence.** If the premise needs a paragraph, it will not survive being
  told through terminals and props.
- **Let the player arrive late.** Aftermath plus evidence is cheaper than live NPC drama and more memorable.
  Budget for skeleton tableaux, signage and three to six short logs, not cutscenes.
- **Map cause to depth.** Put consequences at the door and causes in the last room. Level layout becomes
  story structure for free.
- **Each site needs one bespoke environmental gimmick** layered on the shared kit (flooding, overgrowth, a
  filter on the screen, a single repeated voice line). Without it, modular interiors blur together.
- **End on a turn or a costly choice,** ideally one that reaches outside the site (Vault 34 affecting farms
  and a faction). Sealed stories are stronger when the seal finally breaks in the player's hands.
- **Keep some sites mundane or successful.** Controls and working communities are what make the horrors
  read as horrors, and they double as hubs. Roughly one "it mostly worked" for every four disasters.
- **Decide the institutional motive early and keep it consistent.** Fallout's biggest structural weakness is
  that the reason for the experiments was invented after the first game, never stated in-game by its
  author, and has been re-explained four times. We should know who built our sites, why, and who was meant
  to read the results, even if players only ever see hints.
- **Scale the cruelty to the motive.** Deprivation and demographic tests feel like real research; elaborate
  torture does not. Plausible, bureaucratic harm is more chilling and does not break the setting.
- **Avoid observer-less experiments.** Give at least some sites a surviving data trail, a monitoring
  station, or a living observer, so that uncovering *who was watching* is a meta-plot across sites.
- **A cheerful institutional brand is a force multiplier.** One mascot and one colour scheme, applied
  consistently, supplies irony in every room at no extra writing cost. Ours needs its own visual identity
  and tone, not a 1950s corporate pastiche.
- **Low combat is acceptable, even preferable,** in story sites. The most loved vault has almost none.
- **Do not let the tutorial site be the only "safe" one.** Starting inside the container, then revisiting
  the same architecture as a tomb, is the trick that makes every later site personal.
