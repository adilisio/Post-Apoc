# Fallout Universe — The Pre-War World

How the series builds, satirises and exploits the United States of 2077: the world the bombs ended, and the
franchise's main engine of dark comedy.

Tag key: `[DISPUTED]` · `[RETCON]` · `[SEMI-CANON]` · `[TV]` (Amazon series) · `[UNVERIFIED]` = recalled from
general knowledge of the games and not re-checked against a source in this session (Nukapedia blocked
automated access).

## Quick Reference

- Pre-war America is **1950s culture frozen and extrapolated to 2077**: nuclear cars, robot butlers, tube
  computers, crew cuts, nuclear-family advertising, and a Red Scare aimed at China instead of the USSR.
- The concept dates to late 1995, when art director Leonard Boyarsky proposed a "1950s science-fiction
  future" as the filter for a setting that had been a loose mix of ideas.
- Technology is lopsided by design: fusion power, lasers, AI and power armour coexist with monochrome
  terminals, reel tape and vacuum tubes.
- The state is **authoritarian, militarised and paranoid**: closed borders (2052), martial law (2076),
  internment of Chinese-Americans, wartime censorship, soldiers in power armour firing on food rioters.
- The US was reorganised in 1969 into **13 Commonwealths** above the states; only a few are named in the
  games, and the commonly circulated full list is from a fan RPG `[SEMI-CANON]`.
- The flag carries 13 stars. Per Tim Cain (relayed in the Fallout Bible), Boyarsky chose it because it
  looked good; the "13 super-states" explanation came afterwards.
- **Corporations are the real government.** Nearly every pre-war company in the series is shown lying to,
  experimenting on, or killing its customers and staff, usually under military contract.
- **Vault-Tec** sold salvation and ran social experiments; **the Enclave** — a cabal of officials,
  generals and industrialists — planned to outlive everyone else.
- The pre-war world is almost never shown directly. It is reconstructed from **terminals, holotapes,
  posters, skeleton tableaux and still-cheerful robots** — the ruin is the storytelling medium.
- The comedy formula is **cheerful surface over lethal subtext**: a smiling mascot illustrating radiation
  sickness; a jingle for a soft drink with a radioactive isotope in it.
- The darkest joke is structural: the people who promised safety (Vault-Tec, the government) are the ones
  who did the most harm, and the adverts outlived the customers.
- Bethesda-era games (3, 4, 76) greatly expanded the pre-war world; the Interplay games sketched it in a
  few intro frames and manuals. Fallout 4's playable prologue and the `[TV]` flashbacks are the only places
  we see it alive.
- Shortages drive everything: oil gone by 2060, fuel prices absurd, food and energy riots by 2076 — yet the
  advertising never stops promising abundance.
- Estimated US population by 2077: almost 400 million `[SEMI-CANON]` (Fallout Bible).

## 1. Political geography

### The 13 Commonwealths

Created in 1969 (Fallout 4 placard at the Massachusetts State House). They sat between state and federal
level and reportedly spent their energy competing with each other. Only fragments are canon; the list below
comes from a 2004 fan tabletop RPG partly written by Josh Sawyer, echoed in his *Van Buren* design notes.

### Government and the road to martial law

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Structure | President, Congress, courts all nominally intact; the last president is never named |
| Borders | Closed 5 March 2052 |
| Emergency powers | National quarantine (2053, New Plague); martial law (Aug 2076) |
| Internment | "Executive Order 99066" detains Chinese-Americans without trial — a direct mirror of the real 1942 order; camps appear in Fallout 3/NV/76 |
| Censorship and loyalty | "New Amended Espionage Act"; seizure of "seditious" material; propaganda against "Commies" everywhere |
| Labour | Workers' compensation suspended for the war; mass replacement of workers by robots leads to automation riots (Fallout 76's Appalachia) |
| Annexations | Canada occupied from 2066, annexed 2072–Jan 2076, with summary executions of resisters shown in the Fallout 1 intro; Mexico entered by US troops in 2051 for its oil |
| Economy | Oil exhausted by 2060; hyperinflation implied by in-world price tags (fuel priced in the thousands of dollars per gallon `[UNVERIFIED]`) |
| Final collapse | Food and energy riots; army units used on civilians (Jan 2077); President withdraws to an oil rig (Mar 2077) |

### The Enclave's origins

- A **secret contingent within the continuity-of-government system**: presidents, Joint Chiefs,
  industrialists, generals, politicians and scientists who concluded nuclear war was survivable — for them.
- Secured hardened sites: the Poseidon oil rig off California (Control Station ENCLAVE), Raven Rock, the
  Whitespring congressional bunker in Appalachia.
- Invested in and steered Vault-Tec; treated the Vault programme as "a social experiment on a grand scale".
- **Tim Cain's stated intent** (originating in Fallout 2 development, relayed by Avellone in the Fallout
  Bible and repeated by Cain since): the experiments were to gather data for a multi-generation starship,
  because the Enclave meant to leave Earth. This is never stated in a game `[SEMI-CANON]`.
- In Fallout 1 the Vaults were simply shelters. The experiment conceit arrived with Fallout 2 `[RETCON]`
  and became the franchise's signature idea.
- `[TV]` adds that Vault-Tec management and allied corporations discussed *causing* the war to guarantee
  their product's value, and ties named executives to the Enclave.

## 2. Culture: the World of Tomorrow, 120 years late

| Element | 1950s root | Fallout exaggeration |
|---|---|---|
| Domestic life | Suburban nuclear family, housewife ideal | Mr. Handy robot butler; Fallout 4's Sanctuary Hills prologue |
| Cars | Tail-finned concept cars, "atomic car" studies | Fusion-powered Chryslus models that explode when shot |
| Media | Radio serials, pulp comics, drive-ins | The Silver Shroud, Grognak, Hubris Comics; 1940s–50s pop on every radio |
| Computing | Room-sized mainframes | Green-screen RobCo terminals, wrist-mounted Pip-Boy, holotapes |
| Civil defence | "Duck and cover", backyard shelters | Vault-Tec; Vault Boy's cheery survival pamphlets |
| Anti-communism | McCarthyism | Liberty Prime shouting slogans; loyalty checks; internment camps |
| Atomic optimism | "Atoms for Peace", too-cheap-to-meter | Household reactors, radioactive soda, nuclear hand-held launcher |
| Consumer branding | Mascots and jingles | Vault Boy, Nuka-Cola, Sugar Bombs cereal, Abraxo cleaner |
| Science fiction | *Forbidden Planet*, ray guns, bug-eyed monsters | Laser rifles, Robby-style robots, aliens as a running gag |

Key observations:

- **Social progress is frozen along with the styling.** The setting keeps mid-century gender and workplace
  norms in its advertising while the games themselves are egalitarian in practice — a tension the series
  mostly leaves unexamined.
- **The music does heavy lifting.** Licensed 1940s–50s songs with ironic lyrics supply tone cheaply and
  instantly. (We cannot reuse the songs, but the principle — period-innocent audio over ruin — is portable.)
- **The aesthetic is a filter, not a year.** Designs range from 1930s deco to early-1960s Googie; what
  unites them is naive confidence.

## 3. Corporations

| Company | Business / products | Misdeeds and dark jokes | Main games |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Vault-Tec** | Fallout shelters (Bible: 122 Vaults), Project Safehouse, a university | Most Vaults were unconsented social experiments; only a few "control" Vaults (17 per Bethesda-era material `[UNVERIFIED]`); `[TV]` management discusses starting the war themselves | All |
| **RobCo Industries** | Robots (Protectron, Securitron, Eyebot), terminals and their OS, Pip-Boy, Stealth Boy; founded 2042 by Robert House | Hostile takeovers; military robots; founder privately prepares to rule Las Vegas after the war he predicted | 3, NV, 4, 76 |
| **General Atomics International** | Mr. Handy, Mr. Gutsy, Miss Nanny; co-built Liberty Prime with RobCo | Sells the same chassis as nanny and as flamethrower-armed soldier; its robots keep serving corpses | 3, 4 |
| **West Tek** | Largest defence research contractor: T-51 power armour, laser weapons, FEV | Bioweapon research in breach of promises; human testing on military prisoners at Mariposa; poisoned a town's water with FEV in Appalachia (Huntersville) | 1, 2, 76 |
| **Poseidon Energy** | Energy cartel: oil, power plants, solar (HELIOS One), the oil rig | Front for the Enclave; a power station that is secretly an orbital weapon; cut-corner plants | 2, NV, 4, 76 |
| **Nuka-Cola Corporation** | Dominant soft drink (formula 2044), theme park (opened 2050) | Quantum variant uses a radioactive isotope; weapons work with the military; founder trades favours for life extension and ends as a preserved head `[UNVERIFIED]` details | 3, 4 (Nuka-World), 76 |
| **Med-Tek** | Pharmaceuticals (Mentats, Fixer `[UNVERIFIED]`), labs | Quarantine facility sealed with patients inside; addictive "performance" drugs sold as lifestyle products | 3, 4 |
| **Big MT (Big Mountain)** | Private think-tank/research crater | Invented half the wasteland's monsters; experimented on interned Chinese-Americans; scientists reduced to brains in jars who forgot why | NV (*Old World Blues*) |
| **REPCONN Aerospace** | Rockets, plasma research | Absorbed by RobCo; fuel leaks; its museum tour lies cheerfully about safety | NV |
| **Mass Fusion** | New England's power utility, fusion reactors | Secret military reactor work under its HQ; safety-shortcut memos `[UNVERIFIED]` | 4 |
| **HalluciGen, Inc.** | Chemical crowd-control agents | Tested gases on people; its own building is found gassed | 4 |
| **Chryslus Motors** | Fusion cars (Corvega etc.) | Known-defect cover-ups; cars as roadside bombs | 3, 4 |
| **Wilson Atomatoys** | Children's toys (robot pony) | Retools to make military hardware as the war economy bites | 4 |
| **Hornwright Industrial / Garrahan Mining** | Appalachian mining, excavator power armour | Replace entire workforces with robots, then hire robots to break the resulting strikes | 76 |
| **Atomic Mining Services** | Ultracite mining | Destroys a town via its own extraction technology | 76 |
| **Cambridge Polymer Labs** | Materials research | Management locks staff inside to finish a military contract; they are still there | 4 |
| **H&H Tools** | Tool manufacturer (House family) | Paranoid owner's surveillance and staff "loyalty" regime | NV |
| **Vault-Tec's suppliers** (e.g. Hawthorne Medical) | Lab work for Vault experiments | Human hybridisation experiments `[TV]` | TV |

Pattern across the table: (1) every firm has a **military contract**; (2) every firm has a **mascot or
slogan** that survives it; (3) the evidence is always **an internal memo** — the player is positioned as an
auditor reading the minutes of the apocalypse.

## 4. How the series uses the pre-war world as comedy

| Technique | How it works | Example type |
|---|---|---|
| Cheerful instructional framing | A grinning cartoon explains something horrific | Vault Boy perk and injury illustrations; S.P.E.C.I.A.L. training films |
| Corporate euphemism | Atrocity in HR language | Vault overseer briefing documents; "voluntary" test-subject memos |
| The memo trail | A terminal's entries escalate from petty office feud to mass death | Almost every Bethesda-era office building |
| Loyal machines | Robots continue routines for dead owners | Butlers tending skeleton families; tour guides in ruined museums |
| Skeleton tableaux | Wordless final-moment scenes | Bathtub with toaster; couple embracing; skeleton at a slot machine |
| Brand persistence | 200-year-old products are still the economy | Bottle caps as currency; branded snacks as food |
| Propaganda overreach | Patriotism so loud it parodies itself | A giant robot reciting anti-communist slogans |
| Bureaucratic apocalypse | The end of the world arrives with paperwork | Vault admission lists; a salesman at the door minutes before the sirens |
| Experiment as punchline | Each Vault is a one-line cruel premise | One man and a crate of puppets; a Vault of clones; a Vault that never closed |

Why it works:

- **Distance.** The victims are 200 years dead, so the jokes land as satire of institutions, not mockery of
  suffering in front of the player. When the series shows the pre-war world *alive* (Fallout 4's prologue,
  the show's Cooper Howard flashbacks) the tone shifts to dread and tragedy instead.
- **Complicity.** The audience recognises the advertising voice. The satire is of consumer capitalism and
  Cold War militarism generally, with the retro styling as a safe wrapper.
- **Contrast with the wasteland.** Post-war factions are judged against the pre-war world; anyone trying to
  restore "America" (the Enclave, sometimes the NCR) is implicitly repeating the mistake.

Criticisms worth noting:

- Bethesda-era games are often accused of **letting the pre-war world swallow the setting** — 200 years on,
  people still live among unswept skeletons and eat 2077 snack cakes, which strains plausibility and crowds
  out new post-war culture `[DISPUTED]` as a matter of taste.
- Overuse of the "evil Vault experiment" and "evil corporation memo" formulas makes them predictable; by
  Fallout 76 the reveal that a company was malign carries no surprise.
- The originals used the pre-war world sparingly, which kept it mythic.

## Gaps

- The complete, canonical membership of the 13 Commonwealths does not exist in any game; the table is from
  a fan RPG and design notes.
- Several corporate details (Med-Tek product lines, Mass Fusion specifics, Nuka-Cola founder story, the
  "17 control Vaults" figure, in-world prices) are from memory and tagged `[UNVERIFIED]`.
- No primary Boyarsky interview was read; the origin story comes via summaries of Tim Cain's channel.

## Design Takeaways

- **Build the dead world as carefully as the live one.** Fallout's ruins are interesting because the
  society that left them had a clear ideology, look and set of lies. Write our pre-collapse culture's
  self-image first, then break it.
- **Pick one naive confidence to satirise.** Fallout chose atomic-age consumer optimism. Ours should be a
  *different* era's self-delusion with its own visual language — the technique transfers, the 1950s do not.
- **Tone rule: cheerful surface, lethal subtext.** Codify it for writers. Every pre-collapse artefact
  should be selling something while revealing something worse.
- **Give the old world a voice that outlived it** — a mascot, an institutional register, a jingle style —
  so any prop can deliver exposition and a joke at once.
- **Let institutions be the villains, and document them through their own paperwork.** Player-as-auditor
  is cheap to produce and strongly rewards exploration.
- **Keep the living old world off-screen, or nearly.** Its power is archaeological. One short playable
  glimpse is worth more than a lot of flashback.
- **Ration the formula.** Decide how many "experiment bunkers" or equivalent the whole game gets. If every
  ruin hides an atrocity memo, none of them matter. Mix in mundane, sad and even decent stories.
- **Let the old world decay as time passes.** If our game is set generations later, people should have
  cleaned up, repurposed and mythologised the ruins. New culture must visibly outweigh old branding.
- **Lopsided technology needs a stated rule** (what advanced, what never did) in the setting bible, or
  artists and writers will drift.
- **Derive geography and politics from one or two structural changes** (Fallout: 13 commonwealths, closed
  borders, annexed neighbours) and then actually finish the list — Fallout never did, and fans filled it
  with non-canon material.
- **Do not make the satire a costume for nostalgia.** Fallout's merchandise now sells the brands it
  mocked. Decide whether our old-world iconography is meant to be lovable, and accept the consequence.
