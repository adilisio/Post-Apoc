# Technology and Items

Scope: the technology rules, equipment lineages, consumables, brands and currencies of Fallout 1, 2, 3,
New Vegas, 4 and 76. Descriptive and analytical; no game text reproduced.

## Quick Reference

- Fallout's future is the one the 1950s expected: **atomic power everywhere, robots and ray guns, but
  no microelectronics revolution**. Computers are room-sized or desk-sized, screens are monochrome CRTs,
  storage is tape.
- The vacuum-tube rule was an **art decision first**: Leonard Boyarsky pitched it to Tim Cain because
  things "would look cooler" with tubes; lore justification came after. It has never been applied with
  perfect consistency.
- "The transistor was never invented" is the popular shorthand; fallout.wiki's reading is that it was
  invented but **miniaturisation never took off**. A Bethesda developer has said flatly it was never
  invented. `[DISPUTED]`
- Power comes from **fusion** (microfusion cells, fusion cores, car reactors). Oil ran out, which caused the
  Resource Wars and ultimately the Great War of 23 October 2077.
- **Power armor** is the setting's icon. Lineage: T-45 (2067) -> T-51 (2076) -> T-60 (added by Fallout 4,
  retroactively pre-war) -> X-01 (prototype) -> Enclave Advanced Power Armor (post-war) -> Hellfire.
- Fallout 4 changed power armor from a wearable item needing training into a **piloted frame burning
  fusion cores**. Mechanically praised; lore-wise contested.
- Weapons split into **ballistic** (cheap ammo, common) and **energy** (laser, plasma; rarer ammo, strong
  against robots/armour by game). Black Isle used real gun brands; Bethesda uses invented ones.
- The **Pip-Boy** is the diegetic menu: a RobCo wrist computer. Models: 2000 (FO1/2), 3000 (FO3), 3000A
  (NV), 3000 Mk IV (FO4), 2000 Mk VI (FO76).
- **RobCo** (Protectron, Securitron, Assaultron, eyebots, terminals, Pip-Boys) and **General Atomics**
  (Mister Handy family, robobrain) are the two robot makers.
- **Chems** are consumable buffs with addiction risk: Stimpak, RadAway, Rad-X, Buffout, Mentats, Psycho,
  Jet, Med-X.
- **Med-X was "morphine"** until Australia's classification board refused Fallout 3 a rating in July 2008;
  Bethesda renamed it worldwide. The internal ID is still "Morphine".
- **Bottle caps** are currency because the Hub's water merchants backed them (Fallout 1). Fallout 2 moved
  to NCR coin; New Vegas brought caps back with an economic reason; Bethesda uses them everywhere with
  little explanation.
- The **G.E.C.K.** began as a briefcase of seeds, fertiliser and a library (Fallout 2) and became a
  matter-rearranging miracle box (Fallout 3). `[RETCON]`
- **Nuka-Cola** (invented 2044) is the anchor brand; its caps are the currency, its glowing Quantum variant
  (2077) is a collectible and crafting input.
- Recognisability comes from a **closed visual grammar**: a fixed palette, a mascot, rounded atomic-age
  industrial design, and a short list of forbidden technologies.

## 1. The retro-future rules

### 1.1 What exists and what does not

| Domain | Present | Absent or stunted |
|---|---|---|
| Energy | Fission and fusion at every scale: city plants, car engines, hand-held cells | Late-century oil economy (reserves exhausted by the 2050s-60s) |
| Computing | Mainframes (ZAX series), desk terminals running a text OS, true AI in a few machines | Microprocessors, GUIs, colour displays, mobile phones, a public internet |
| Displays | Monochrome phosphor CRTs (green or amber), nixie tubes, analogue gauges | Flat panels, touch screens |
| Storage | Holotapes / holodisks (cassette-like), punch cards, reel tape | Solid-state media |
| Robotics | Autonomous household and military robots; brain-in-a-jar cyborgs; androids (Institute, post-war) | Networked, miniaturised drones (mostly) |
| Weapons | Lasers, plasma, Gauss, mini-nukes, powered armour | Guided "smart" munitions as a norm |
| Medicine | Auto-docs, instant-healing injectors, cryogenics, cloning, cybernetics | - |
| Transport | Fusion cars with fins, vertibirds (tilt-rotor VTOL), monorails | Mass civil aviation is barely shown |
| Media | Radio, black-and-white TV, comics, pulp magazines | Anything after roughly 1960 in style |
| Culture | 1950s Americana frozen for 120 years: nuclear family advertising, Red Scare politics | Counterculture, civil-rights-era and later aesthetics |

### 1.2 How the rule was made

| Source | Claim |
|---|---|
| Leonard Boyarsky (art director, Fallout 1), via fallout.wiki | Proposed that the world stayed with vacuum tubes; motive was visual. Cain added the rationalisation that tube electronics resist EMP |
| Tim Cain | The setting follows "the world of tomorrow" as imagined in the 1950s, including its understanding of radiation |
| J.E. Sawyer (2003) | Noted the inconsistency: EMP/pulse grenades are devastating to robots, which a tube-based world would not predict |
| Joel Burgess (Bethesda, 2016 livestream Q&A) | Said transistors were never invented in the Fallout world |
| Fan timelines | Often cite the transistor arriving c. 2067 `[UNVERIFIED]`; fallout.wiki frames it as invented but never miniaturised |

Designer's reading: this is not a physics model, it is a **style guide with a list of bans**. The bans (no
flat screens, no sleek plastic, no touch UI) do more work than the lore explanation.

### 1.3 The shift between eras

| | Interplay / Black Isle (1997-98) | Bethesda (2008-) | Obsidian (2010) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dominant reference | 1950s pulp SF filtered through 1980s-90s post-apocalypse (Mad Max leather, punk raiders) | 1950s consumer culture foregrounded: diners, suburbs, jingles, mascots everywhere | Western and Rat Pack Vegas layered on the Black Isle tone |
| Pre-war world | Mostly implied; a grim militarised state | Heavily depicted and satirised | Corporate history (House, RobCo, REPCONN) |
| Guns | Real brands plus invented energy weapons | All invented, chunky, stylised | Real-world-derived designs under generic names |
| Colour | Brown/grey isometric | Green tint (FO3), saturated blues and reds (FO4), lush forest (76) | Orange desert |

A long-running fan argument is whether Fallout's world *is* culturally stuck in the 1950s or merely has
a future that *looks like* the 1950s' predictions. The original games lean to the latter; Bethesda's
depiction leans to the former.

### 2.2 The mechanical break in Fallout 4

| Aspect | Fallout 1, 2, 3, NV | Fallout 4, 76 |
|---|---|---|
| Object type | An armour item you equip | An entered exoskeleton frame with six detachable armour pieces |
| Requirement | Training (a story reward or perk in 3/NV); strength boost | None; anyone can climb in |
| Power | Internal micro-fusion pack, stated in Fallout 1 to last about a century | Fusion cores that drain in minutes of sprinting `[RETCON]` |
| When obtained | Late-game reward (FO1 via a Brotherhood quest; FO2 at Navarro; FO3 near the end) | First hour (a scripted rooftop fight against a deathclaw) |
| Feel | A stat upgrade | A vehicle: HUD cockpit, heavy footfalls, fall-damage immunity, repair station |

Reception: almost everyone agrees the Fallout 4 suit *feels* right for the first time (weight, sound,
scale), and that making it fuel-limited is a good loop. The objections are that it is handed out too
early, that fusion-core drain contradicts earlier lore, and that removing training erases the Brotherhood's
mystique. Fallout 76 keeps the system but makes cores abundant, effectively dropping the fuel constraint.

### 2.3 Why power armor is the franchise face

It is a single silhouette that says "retro" (riveted, tank-like), "future" (powered exoskeleton) and
"military failure" (the best armour ever built did not save anyone). Every box cover except New Vegas's
uses a helmet.

## 3. Weapons: energy versus ballistic

### 3.1 Families

| Family | Examples (generic descriptions) | Ammo | Design niche |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pistols / SMGs / rifles | 10 mm sidearm (starter in most games), .44 revolver, hunting rifle, assault rifles, combat shotgun, sniper rifle | Calibre-specific rounds | Cheap, plentiful, the baseline |
| Improvised | Pipe guns (FO4/76), railway-spike launcher, junk launcher, nail-board, tyre-iron | Scrap | Establishes scavenger tone and early-game weakness |
| Heavy ballistic | Minigun, .50 cal, missile launcher, shoulder-fired mini-nuke catapult | 5 mm, missiles, mini-nukes | Power fantasy; ammo-starved |
| Laser | Pistol, rifle, Gatling laser, multi-beam variants, Institute pattern | Energy/fusion cells | Accurate, no travel time, weak per shot; turns targets to ash |
| Plasma | Pistol, rifle, "caster" | Plasma cartridges / microfusion cells | Slow projectile, high damage; turns targets to goo |
| Electromagnetic | Gauss rifle (a coilgun; counted as energy or ballistic depending on the game), pulse weapons, Tesla cannon | 2 mm EC, cells | Late-game sniping; anti-robot |
| Exotic | Alien blaster, cryo thrower, flamer, radiation ("gamma") gun, sonic and holographic guns in NV DLC | Unique | Collectible uniques |
| Melee / unarmed | Powered fist, chainsaw-knife, rocket sledgehammer, flaming sword | - | Full build path in every game |

### 3.2 How the split has been tuned

| Game | Skill structure | Balance verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Fallout 1-2 | Small Guns / Big Guns / Energy Weapons | Energy is strictly late-game best-in-class (turbo plasma, Gauss). Big Guns notoriously under-supported in FO1 |
| Fallout 3 | Same three skills | Energy available early; iconic uniques; little systemic difference beyond ammo type |
| New Vegas | Guns / Energy Weapons / Explosives; ammo sub-types and crafting, damage-threshold armour | Most systemic: armour penetration matters. Energy widely felt to be weaker than Guns until patches and the Gun Runners' Arsenal DLC |
| Fallout 4 | Skills removed; perks by weapon *action* (rifleman, commando) so energy and ballistic share perks; armour has separate ballistic and energy resistances | Deep modular crafting. Energy is a damage-type choice, not a build. Enemies usually resist one type more than the other |
| Fallout 76 | Fallout 4 model plus legendary effects | Meta driven by legendary rolls, not weapon class |

### 3.3 Art-direction notes

- **Black Isle** mixed real firearms (named after real manufacturers) with invented energy weapons. This
  grounded the world but created licensing and tone issues.
- **Bethesda** invented everything. Fallout 4's chunky water-jacketed "assault rifle", designed to look
  right in power-armoured hands, is widely mocked as impractical when carried without the suit.
- **Obsidian** used recognisable real-world patterns with generic names ("service rifle", "9 mm pistol").
- **The mini-nuke launcher** (Fallout 3) is a tone marker: gleefully absurd, and the Japanese release
  renamed it because its original name matched a real atomic bomb `[UNVERIFIED specifics]`.
- **Critical-kill effects** (ash piles, green goo) give energy weapons identity for almost no cost.
- **V.A.T.S.**, the pause-and-target system, is the mechanical bridge from the turn-based originals and
  the showcase for these weapons; see the combat research files.

## 4. The Pip-Boy

| Model | Game | Form | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pip-Boy 2000 | Fallout 1, 2 (also Tactics) | Presented as the game's interface panel; later lore treats it as wrist-worn `[DISPUTED as originally a handheld]` | Status, automap, quest log (the "Pip-Boy" is the mascot-like figure on the device, modelled on a diner-chain mascot per Boyarsky, distinct from Vault Boy) |
| Pip-Boy 3000 | Fallout 3 | Bulky wrist unit, green screen, bio-locked to the wearer | Three tabs (Stats / Items / Data), radio, torch, Geiger counter, V.A.T.S. host |
| Pip-Boy 3000A | New Vegas | Same shell, amber screen by default | A gift from a doctor rather than vault issue |
| Pimp-Boy 3 Billion | New Vegas | Gold and jewelled reskin | Quest reward; a joke that proves the device is a status object |
| Pip-Boy 3000 Mark IV | Fallout 4 | Slimmer, with a physical holotape deck and visible cassette loading | Plays mini-games on tapes; the character's hand visibly works the dials. A real wearable shell shipped with the collector's edition (2015) |
| Pip-Boy 2000 Mark VI | Fallout 76 | Boxier, rotary dial, kit-bashed look | Chronologically earliest wrist model; photomode and skins |
| Prototype units | TV series and concept art | - | Outside the six games |

Standard functions (per the RPG rulebook summary on fallout.wiki): vital-sign monitor, inventory log, map
and compass, radio receiver, holotape reader, Geiger counter, light.

Why it matters: it turns the pause menu into a prop the character owns. Inventory, map and stats are all
"in the world", in the setting's colour and typeface. It is also the series' most merchandised object.

## 5. Robots and their makers

| Maker | Lines | Character |
|---|---|---|
| **RobCo Industries** (founder: the future ruler of New Vegas) | Protectron (general-purpose biped), eyebot, Securitron (NV), Assaultron (FO4), Stealth Boy cloaking device, Pip-Boy, the standard terminal operating system; partner on the giant war robot | Software and mass-market robotics; boxy, industrial |
| **General Atomics International** | Mister Handy (household), Mister Gutsy (military), Miss Nanny, medical and farm variants; robobrain; partner on the giant war robot | Spherical, three-armed hovering units with chatty personalities; "appliance" feel |
| **US military / contractors** | Sentry bot, turrets, vertibots, the giant war robot | Heavy, olive drab |
| **The Institute** (post-war) | Synths generations 1-3; the third is biologically human | Clean white plastic: deliberately breaks the visual rules to mark them as alien to the setting |
| **Big MT** (NV DLC) | Personality-equipped appliances, robo-scorpions | Mad-science parody |
| **The Mechanist / player** (FO4 Automatron) | Modular custom robots | Part-swapping crafting system |

Design functions of robots: (1) enemies immune to morale and plausibly still guarding after 200 years;
(2) comic relief through cheerful pre-war programming in ruins; (3) hackable allies, rewarding the science
skill; (4) carriers of pre-war ideology (jingoistic military bots).

### The Med-X / morphine incident

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| 4 July 2008 (reported about a week later) | Australia's Classification Board refuses Fallout 3 classification, which bans sale. The stated problem: a real-world drug (morphine) used with a visible positive gameplay incentive, contrary to the code's guidance on drug use related to rewards. Australia had no adult (R18+) games category then |
| August 2008 | A revised version is classified MA15+ with a drug-reference advisory |
| September 2008 | Bethesda's Pete Hines confirms the game will be identical in all territories; real drug references removed, with morphine becoming "Med-X" |
| After | The editor ID stays "Morphine" in Fallout 3 and New Vegas. Fictional names for all chems become firm franchise policy |

Lesson already drawn by the franchise: invented drug names sidestep ratings bodies and are better branding
anyway. (Other chems were already fictional, which is why only one item needed changing.)

How the brand layer works: (1) **repetition** of a dozen logos makes any ruin legible as a place people
shopped and worked; (2) **satire**: nearly every company has a terminal revealing negligence or a military
contract; (3) **mechanical hooks**: brands are healing items, currencies, crafting inputs and collectible
sets, so players actually read the labels.

## 8. Currencies

| Currency | Where / game | In-world logic | Gameplay note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bottle caps (Nuka-Cola) | Fallout 1, California | The Hub's merchant houses back caps against water; chosen because they are durable, scarce, and hard to forge. Designer Scott Campbell has said they were picked as a common, plausible token | Weightless; universal |
| NCR dollars (gold coin) | Fallout 2 | A real state mints gold-backed coin; caps are explicitly worthless (a famous gag stash of thousands of caps) | Signals civilisation recovering |
| Ring pulls; Brotherhood scrip | Fallout Tactics | Regional alternative; faction-issued scrip | `[SEMI-CANON]` |
| Bottle caps | Fallout 3, 4 (East Coast) | Essentially unexplained beyond convention; trade links to the west are sometimes inferred `[DISPUTED]` | Bethesda treats caps as franchise iconography |
| Caps + NCR paper dollars + Legion coin | New Vegas | Per J.E. Sawyer: Brotherhood raids destroyed the NCR's gold reserves, forcing fiat paper; merchants lost confidence and Hub houses reissued water-backed caps. The Legion mints silver and gold coin. Fan-wiki rates: a NCR $100 note is about 40 caps; a Legion denarius 4 caps; an aureus 100 caps | Three currencies with exchange rates dramatise the faction war in the shop menu. Caravan firms control cap presses; a quest involves destroying a counterfeiting press |
| Sierra Madre chips | NV Dead Money | Casino chips redeemable at vending machines | DLC-local economy; a self-contained survival loop |
| Pre-war money | 3, NV, 4 | Worthless as currency; valued as paper/cloth or a trade good | A barter item |
| Caps | Fallout 76 | Unexplained so soon after the war (a resort-promotion tie-in is offered for one location); player-to-player trade | Hard cap on holdings to control inflation |
| Gold bullion, treasury notes | 76 Wastelanders | Pre-war gold reserves from Vault 79 | Time-gated endgame currency |
| Legendary scrip, stamps, tickets, event tokens | 76 | Pure game-system currencies | Live-service gating |
| Barter | All | Direct item swap is always available in 1/2 and NV | The original games are barter-first with caps as one more good |

Observations:

- Caps are a **brilliant diegetic choice**: a piece of pre-war litter that is uniform, countable, finite
  and useless for anything else, and that ties money to the setting's flagship brand.
- The original logic is local (one city's merchants). Each later use weakens it; **Fallout 2 dropping caps
  and New Vegas explaining their return are the only two entries that treat money as worldbuilding**.
- Multiple currencies with fluctuating trust (NV) communicate politics more efficiently than dialogue.

## 9. The G.E.C.K.

| Version | Source | Description |
|---|---|---|
| Original | Fallout 2 (1998) and Fallout Bible | The Garden of Eden Creation Kit: a suitcase-sized vault-issue package for rebuilding after the door opens. Seeds, soil supplements, a small fusion generator, basic fabrication tools, and a library of knowledge on holodisk, with advice to cannibalise the vault for materials. Vault 8 used one to found Vault City. It is the MacGuffin a tribal village sends the player to find |
| Bethesda | Fallout 3 (2008) | A device (credited to a Vault-Tec scientist who also runs Vault 112) that breaks down nearby matter and recombines it into a fertile landscape; lethal to anyone activating it by hand. Needed to complete a water-purification project `[RETCON]` |
| Later | Fallout 76 | Vault 94's unit malfunctions and floods a region with mutant flora, consistent with the Fallout 3 version; a G.E.C.K.-branded crafting station also appears in Vault 88 lore |
| Real-world | 2008-2010 | Bethesda named its Fallout 3 / New Vegas modding toolset the "Garden of Eden Creation Kit" |

The G.E.C.K. is the setting's one piece of pure hope technology. Its change from "a very good starter kit"
to "terraforming magic" is a small-scale example of the series' general drift from plausible to
spectacular.

## 11. Why consistent rules make the setting recognisable

A single screenshot of any Fallout game is identifiable in under a second. The components:

| Layer | Rule | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Technology bans | No flat screens, no thin plastics, no touch interfaces, no modern fonts | Removes the "generic near-future" look that dates other SF |
| Shape language | Rounded corners, fins, rivets, bakelite, enamel, visible valves and dials | Everything looks manufactured by the same civilisation |
| Palette | Vault blue and yellow; phosphor green or amber UI; rust and teal ruins; red rocket accents | Brand colours recur at every scale from logo to landscape |
| Typography and UI | Monospaced terminal type; chunky condensed signage; the wrist-computer frame | The interface is part of the fiction |
| Mascot | One cartoon figure illustrating every stat and perk | Tone (cheerful menace) is delivered in every menu |
| Audio | Mid-century popular songs on in-world radio against ambient dread | Ironic counterpoint without writing a word |
| Brand set | About a dozen fictional companies repeated everywhere | Density of world with a small asset list |
| Material contrast | Optimistic artefacts in decayed spaces | The core image: the future that did not happen |
| Deliberate exceptions | The Institute's white synthetics; Enclave black armour; alien chrome | Breaking the rules *marks* a faction as other. It only works because the rules are otherwise kept |

The underlying principle: **constraints, not content, create identity.** Fallout's list of what cannot
appear is short, strictly policed, and visible in every asset. Controversies (T-60, X-01, pre-war Jet,
fusion cores, the G.E.C.K.) arise almost exclusively when a new instalment breaks a *lore* rule; the
*visual* rules have been kept for nearly thirty years, which is why the brand survived three studios.

## Design Takeaways

- **Write a technology ban list on day one.** Three to six things that cannot exist in our world, chosen
  for look and tone first and rationalised second. Enforce it in every asset review. Ours must be a
  different divergence and a different era-fetish from Fallout's 1950s atomic age.
- **Pick one historical "imagined future" or aesthetic lineage and commit.** Half-commitment yields generic
  post-apocalypse. Do not pick mid-century Americana; that territory is taken.
- **Make the menu a prop.** A diegetic device for inventory, map and stats, in the world's own palette and
  typeface, is the cheapest immersion multiplier Fallout has. Design ours as an object with a manufacturer
  and model history, but not a wrist computer with a monochrome screen.
- **Have one hero silhouette.** Fallout's is the power-armour helmet. Identify ours early and put it on
  everything; protect its in-world rarity.
- **If a signature item is a vehicle, treat it as one.** Fallout 4's frame-and-fuel model is the right
  instinct mechanically. Its mistakes: giving it away in the first hour and making the fuel either too
  scarce (4) or irrelevant (76). Late acquisition plus a meaningful upkeep loop is the target.
- **Do not add "even better than the best" gear retroactively.** If the fiction names a pinnacle, later
  additions should be sidegrades, specialised variants or post-collapse inventions, not older and better.
- **Make damage types a real choice** (penetration, target class, ammo economy), as New Vegas did, rather
  than a cosmetic one. Give each type a distinct kill effect and sound.
- **Decide on real versus invented firearms and stay there.** Invented is safer legally and tonally, but
  designs must look usable in a normal person's hands.
- **Fictional names for all drugs and medicines, from the start.** It avoids ratings problems and builds
  brand. Keep the addiction-versus-buff trade-off; it is a compact risk mechanic.
- **Build a small fictional brand set (10-15) and reuse it relentlessly.** Give each brand a logo, a
  product players use, and a secret. Make labels matter mechanically (healing, crafting, collecting).
- **Currency should be a piece of the old world with a reason.** State who backs it and why it is hard to
  forge. Consider more than one currency if factions are central; exchange rates are free storytelling.
  Do not use bottle caps or any soda-brand token.
- **Money should change as society does.** Fallout 2's move to coin said "civilisation is back" in one
  shop screen. Plan what our economy looks like at different distances from the collapse.
- **Have exactly one hope technology** and keep it modest. A plausible toolkit is more moving than a magic
  terraformer, and it leaves the work of rebuilding to people.
- **Prefer authored uniques to rolled loot for memorability;** use procedural modifiers as a supplement,
  not a replacement.
- **Reserve rule-breaking aesthetics for factions that should feel alien.** One or two deliberate
  violations are powerful; more than that and the rules stop reading as rules.
- **Keep an internal lore ledger of dates and "firsts".** Most Fallout controversies are date collisions
  that a shared timeline document would have caught.
