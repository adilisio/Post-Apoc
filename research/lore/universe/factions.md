# Factions of the Fallout Series (Fallout 1, 2, 3, New Vegas, 4, 76)

Scope: the six mainline/major games. Fallout Tactics, Brotherhood of Steel (console), Van Buren, the tabletop
games and the 2024- TV series are mentioned only where they change how a faction is read, and are flagged.
In-universe dates are given as the wikis report them; where sources disagree this is marked.

## Quick Reference

- Fallout factions fall into five recurring archetypes: **pre-war remnants** (Enclave, Mr. House, Institute),
  **tech monastics** (Brotherhood), **new states** (NCR, Legion, Vault City), **mutual-aid groups**
  (Followers, Responders, Minutemen, Railroad) and **predators** (raiders, Gunners, Talon, Khans).
- The best-regarded factions (NCR, House, the Master, West Coast Brotherhood) each have **a real argument**:
  a problem they correctly identify, a solution that partly works, and a cost the player can go and see.
- The worst-regarded factions fail in one of three ways: **no argument** (Talon Company, generic raiders),
  **an argument the game never shows** (Legion's "safe roads", told but not seen), or **goals that do not add
  up** (the Institute replacing farmers with synths; nobody in the game can say why).
- **Material basis matters.** NCR is believable because the game shows its brahmin barons, taxes, water-backed
  currency, conscripts and supply lines. The Minutemen and the Institute have almost none of this on screen.
- **Internal disagreement** is the cheapest way to make a faction feel real. Sawyer's stated rule: a faction
  "contains multitudes" - members argue with each other (Hanlon vs. Oliver, McNamara vs. Hardin).
- The Brotherhood is the series' most-reused faction and its most-rewritten: isolationist monks (FO1),
  irrelevant relic (FO2), heroic knights (FO3), dying bunker cult (NV), techno-fascist army (FO4), founding
  myth (76). Every rewrite is explained in-fiction as a schism.
- The Enclave is the series' cleanest pure villain; it works in FO2 because it is *the pre-war government
  that caused everything*, and works less well each time it is reused as generic power-armoured enemies.
- Caesar's Legion is the standard case study for "strong concept, thin implementation": a celebrated leader
  dialogue, but only military camps on the map and most planned territory cut for time.
- The Institute is the standard case study for "strong aesthetic, incoherent motive".
- The Minutemen are the standard case study for "a faction that is only a gameplay system" (settlements +
  radiant quests) with no politics, no dissent and no cost.
- Fallout 76 launched with **every faction already dead** (told through holotapes); Wastelanders (April 2020)
  added living Settlers and Raiders. Dead factions gave good tragedy but no reactivity.
- Raiders are characterised well only when they have a **name, a history and an economy** (Khans, Powder
  Gangers, Fiends' chem trade, Nuka-World gangs). Fallout 3's raiders are scenery.
- West Coast games treat super mutants and ghouls as **people with politics**; East Coast games mostly treat
  super mutants as orcs, with one "good exception" companion per game.
- Ghoul towns are the series' recurring vehicle for **prejudice stories** (Necropolis, Gecko vs. Vault City,
  Tenpenny Tower, Diamond City's 2282 expulsion).
- The "post-apocalyptic vs. post-post-apocalyptic" split: Interplay/Obsidian games let societies rebuild over
  time; Bethesda's games (and the TV series' destruction of the NCR capital) keep resetting to ruins.
- Yes Man / independent endings give freedom but, per New Vegas's lead writer, let players finish
  "without getting your hands dirty" - a faction-free route weakens the faction dilemma.
- Tim Cain (2025) said he always wanted "an actual good faction" that others *distrust for being good*;
  the series has gestured at it (Followers, Responders, Minutemen) but never made it a dilemma.

## 1. Brotherhood of Steel

**Creator and inspiration.** Designed for Fallout 1 by R. Scott Campbell, who said he "simply wanted a group
exactly like the monks from the Guardian Citadel in *Wasteland*" and "loved the idea of high-tech knights in
power-armor" (quoted via Wikipedia). Commonly cited further influences are medieval military-monastic orders
(Templars/Hospitallers) and the knowledge-preserving monks of *A Canticle for Leibowitz*; the latter is fan
and critic consensus rather than a quote I could verify `[UNVERIFIED as developer statement]`.

**Origin.** Captain Roger Maxson, US Army, was posted to the West Tek research programme and then Mariposa
Military Base (January 2077). On discovering that scientists were testing the Forced Evolutionary Virus on
military prisoners, his men executed the scientists; Maxson declared the unit seceded from the Army on
20 October 2077, three days before the war. The survivors and their families marched to the Lost Hills
bunker and Maxson recast the unit as a quasi-religious order so that it would outlive its founders.

**Ideology.** Humanity destroyed itself with technology it was not wise enough to hold; therefore advanced
technology must be collected and kept by a disciplined order. The founding texts are the **Codex** and (named
in New Vegas) the **Chain That Binds** - orders flow strictly down the chain of command and may not skip a
link. The core contradiction is built in: an order founded in revulsion at military abuse of science became
a military that hoards science.

**Ranks.**

| Tier | West Coast (FO1/FO2/NV) | East Coast (FO3/FO4) |
|---|---|---|
| Entry | Initiate | Squire (children), Initiate, Aspirant |
| Craft / science | Scribe (Apprentice, Journeyman, Senior, Head) | Scribe, Senior Scribe, Proctor (heads of orders in FO4) |
| Arms / engineering | Knight (Apprentice, Journeyman, Senior, Head) | Knight, Knight-Sergeant, Knight-Captain, Knight-Commander; Lancer ranks for aircrew (FO4) |
| Elite | Paladin (Junior, Senior, Head) | Paladin, Paladin-Commander, Star Paladin, Sentinel |
| Command | Elder, Elder Council, High Elder | Elder (single autocratic leader per chapter) |

Note the design shift: in FO1 Knights *build* weapons and Paladins *use* them; by FO3/FO4 "Knight" is simply
the infantry rank.

**Relations.** Trading partner then rival of NCR (the NCR-Brotherhood War over technology control, which
the Brotherhood lost on numbers); arch-enemy of the Enclave (FO2, FO3) and the Institute (FO4); contemptuous
of the Followers' open-knowledge creed; hunts super mutants everywhere.

**Why it lasts.** The Brotherhood is a *structure* (monastic military + tech taboo) that can host any
ideology from charity to fascism, so each writer can re-point it via a schism. That flexibility is also why
critics complain it is overused: it appears in every game regardless of geography. Windows Central's Brendan
Lowry argued the FO4 Brotherhood is "the only faction ... that makes you critically think" in that game.

## 2. The Enclave

**Origin.** The pre-war United States' continuity-of-government apparatus: politicians, generals, and
corporate allies (Vault-Tec, Poseidon Energy) who planned to sit out the war. The president relocated to a
Poseidon oil rig in the Pacific in March 2077. The Vault experiment programme is retroactively framed as the
Enclave's research into how to manage a population.

**Ideology.** Only those sheltered under Enclave control are "pure" humans; everyone on the mainland is a
mutant to be exterminated so that "real America" can resume. A fascist bureaucracy that still holds
elections and uses the language of patriotism.

**Structure.** President; vice president; Secret Service (Frank Horrigan, a mutated cyborg agent); a military
with conventional US ranks; science divisions. Appalachia adds a cabinet (Secretary of Agriculture Thomas
Eckhart seizes the presidency) and an AI caretaker, MODUS.

| Game | Date | Base | Leader | Portrayal |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| FO2 | 2241-42 | Oil rig; Navarro refuelling base | President Dick Richardson | The reveal villain. Kidnaps Vault 13 and Arroyo as test subjects, excavates Mariposa for FEV (2236), plans airborne FEV genocide. Destroyed with the rig in 2242. Satirical: a smiling politician explaining extermination |
| FO3 | 2277 | Raven Rock; mobile crawler at Adams AFB (Broken Steel) | "President" John Henry Eden (an AI) and Colonel Augustus Autumn | Soothing radio propaganda over a wasteland it barely governs. Eden wants to poison the purifier with modified FEV; Autumn wants to rule conventionally - a leadership split the game under-uses |
| NV | 2281 | None (Remnants bunker) | Judah Kreger | Six ageing veterans of Navarro living in hiding. Arcade Gannon's quest lets you send them to fight for either side or neither. Widely considered the most human Enclave writing: old soldiers with nostalgia and guilt |
| FO4 | 2287 | - | - | Absent as an organisation; survives as equipment |
| 76 | 2077-2086 | Whitespring bunker | Eckhart, then MODUS | Eckhart deliberately engineered crises (releasing super mutants, breeding scorchbeasts) to trip automated war-readiness thresholds and gain nuclear launch access. The faction murdered itself in a coup/counter-coup; the player "joins" a dead organisation run by a damaged AI and rises to General |

**Technology.** Tier 4: post-war power armour designs, Vertibirds, plasma weapons, orbital strike relay,
cold fusion. Its tech lead is the entire basis of its threat - numbers are always small.

**Contradictions.** Claims to *be* America while planning to kill all Americans; preaches purity while its
champion (Horrigan) and its East Coast president (a computer) are not human; depends on trading with New
Reno mobsters for chemicals.

**Relations.** Enemy of everyone; covertly supplies the Salvatore family in FO2; destroyed twice by the
Brotherhood and once by NCR at Navarro.

**Inspiration.** Cold War bunker-government planning, 1990s *X-Files* shadow-government paranoia, eugenic
fascism dressed in 1950s Americana.

**Portrayal drift.** FO2's Enclave is a thematic statement (the people who ended the world are still in
charge of a piece of it). FO3's is mostly an enemy type. 76 rehabilitates it as a horror story told through
logs. The lesson: a pure villain faction works once, as a reveal.

## 3. New California Republic (NCR)

**Origin.** Shady Sands, a farming village founded by Vault 15 emigrants (FO1). The village elder Aradesh
and his daughter Tandi built it into a federation. Founding date is 2186 in the Fallout Bible and 2189 on
current wikis `[DISPUTED]`. Five founding states: Shady Sands, Los Angeles, Maxson, the Hub, Dayglow.

**Ideology.** Restoration of old-world values: democracy, rule of law, citizenship, taxes. In practice,
expansionist and annexationist.

**Structure.** President and vice president, a bicameral-ish Congress/Senate, courts; presidency was
effectively a Tandi monarchy for 52 years (to 2248), followed by weaker successors and then the hawkish
war hero Aaron Kimball. Military: volunteer-and-conscript Army (troopers), elite Rangers (absorbed the
Nevada Desert Rangers by treaty in 2271), First Recon snipers, heavy troopers in salvaged power armour with
the servos removed (they cannot train enough operators - a nice material detail).

**Economy (the reason it convinces).** Large-scale agriculture and brahmin ranching controlled by "brahmin
barons" who buy politicians; merchant houses (Crimson Caravan, Gun Runners); a paper NCR dollar that lost
its gold backing when the Brotherhood attacked the reserves, now trading well below the bottle cap; a
population figure of ~700,000 given for the FO2 era (Fallout Bible) and "over 700,000" on current wikis.

| Game | Portrayal |
|---|---|
| FO1 (2161) | Pre-history: Shady Sands, the first quest hub, with Tandi as a rescuable teenager |
| FO2 (2241) | A walled, slightly smug capital with police, a slavery ban, anti-mutant prejudice in the streets, and a foreign policy of destabilising neighbours (covertly backing the Bishops' raids to push Vault City into annexation). The first "big state" in the series - impressive and already a bit sinister |
| NV (2281) | The fullest portrait of any Fallout faction: an overextended occupier of the Mojave holding Hoover Dam. The game shows the whole stack - bored conscripts, a glory-seeking General Oliver, a disillusioned Chief Ranger Hanlon falsifying intelligence to force a withdrawal, a ruthless Colonel Moore, an ineffective embassy, sharecroppers who get water while locals do not, a prison break (Powder Gangers) and the Bitter Springs massacre (2278) |
| FO3 / FO4 | Referenced only |
| TV series `[DISPUTED]` | The capital Shady Sands is shown destroyed; an on-screen timeline reading "2277" for its "fall" caused a continuity argument, later clarified by Bethesda staff as the start of a decline with the bombing after 2281 |

**Contradictions.** A democracy run by ranchers; an anti-slavery state using prison labour; a liberator the
locals did not ask for; an army too large to pay for and too thin to hold what it has taken. Its Mojave
treaty gives the Strip free power and no tax obligation, so the occupation runs at a loss.

**Relations.** At war with the Legion; cold peace with Mr. House (Treaty of New Vegas, 2274); has beaten the
Brotherhood in the west; alienated the Followers; exterminated/dispersed the Khans; absorbed Vault City and
New Reno by pressure `[SEMI-CANON for the later status]`.

**Inspiration.** The 19th-century United States (manifest destiny, cattle barons, the cavalry) and, in New
Vegas, 2000s American overseas occupation.

## 4. Caesar's Legion

**Origin.** Edward Sallow, an NCR-born scholar raised by the Followers, was sent east around 2246 with a
physician (Bill Calhoun) and met Mormon missionary Joshua Graham. Captured by the Blackfoot tribe, Sallow
taught them total war, took control (2247), and began conquering and dissolving neighbouring tribes -
86 by Caesar's own count in the game, 87 on the wiki `[DISPUTED]`.

**Ideology.** Sallow read Roman history and Hegel in Follower libraries and concluded that the tribes needed
a single imposed identity. He frames the Legion as the **antithesis** to NCR's corrupt thesis; their clash
at Hoover Dam should produce a synthesis - a nomad army becoming a settled empire with New Vegas as its
Rome. The individual has no value beyond usefulness to the whole.

**Structure.**

| Rank | Role |
|---|---|
| Caesar | Absolute ruler, worshipped as son of Mars |
| Legate (Lanius; formerly Graham, the burned "Malpais Legate") | Field commander |
| Centurion | Commands a centuria (~80) |
| Decanus | Squad leader (contubernium, ~8) |
| Legionary: veteran / prime / recruit | Line infantry; recruits go in first as expendable |
| Praetorian guard | Caesar's unarmed-combat bodyguard |
| Frumentarii (Vulpes Inculta) | Spies and saboteurs |
| Slaves; priestesses; women as breeders | Non-combatant base |

**Territory and economy.** Arizona, New Mexico and parts of Colorado and Utah; capital at Flagstaff. A
slave economy with minted silver and gold coin (denarius, aureus). Chems and alcohol banned; modern medicine
distrusted. The Legion's one material *argument* - made by trader Dale Barton and by Raul - is that its
roads are completely safe for caravans, unlike NCR's.

**Technology.** Level 1-2 by doctrine: machetes, thrown spears, scavenged rifles, football-pad armour.
Caesar deliberately keeps his soldiers from depending on technology, but privately uses an Auto-Doc and
needs brain surgery the Legion's own beliefs cannot provide - the faction's sharpest contradiction.

**Appearance.** New Vegas only (plus referenced in the TV series). All the player sees are frontier war
camps: the Fort, Cottonwood Cove, the crucified town of Nipton, Nelson.

**Contradictions.** A personality cult with no succession plan (Caesar has a tumour; Lanius is a butcher);
an anti-technology army led by a man kept alive by machines; a "civilisation" that has produced no visible
civilians; a philosophy only its founder understands.

**Inspiration.** Imperial Rome as popularly imagined, Kurtz in *Apocalypse Now* (Avellone, for the original
Van Buren concept), sword-and-sandal films. Lead writer John Gonzalez said he wanted to "present a robust
argument for authoritarianism" so the tyrant would be a serious threat.

**Critical standing.** Split. Caesar's dialogue is repeatedly singled out (Eurogamer: "terrifyingly sane";
Rock Paper Shotgun: among "Obsidian's finest writing"). But the faction around him is the series' textbook
case of underdevelopment: few quests, almost no sympathetic rank-and-file, and no depiction of the pacified
interior that is supposed to justify it. Obsidian staff have said more Legion locations and quests were cut
for time; statements attributed to Sawyer say those areas would have shown ordinary non-Legion residents of
Legion land `[UNVERIFIED - secondary quotation only]`. An Oxford-published classics article (J. T.
Christopher) argues the Legion is a Roman facade over a brutality that does not resemble Rome on inspection
- which is arguably the intended reading.

## 5. Mr. House and New Vegas

**Origin.** Robert House, pre-war founder of the robotics giant RobCo, calculated that war was coming and
fitted Las Vegas with missile defences and himself with life support. His final software upgrade (the
Platinum Chip) arrived a day late, so the defences worked only partly: the city was largely spared but he
spent decades incapacitated. When NCR scouts appeared at Hoover Dam (c. 2274) he woke his Securitron robots,
recruited three local tribes and rebuilt the Strip in seven years.

**Ideology.** Technocratic autocracy. Democracy and ideology are noise; a single competent owner should run
the city as a business, skim NCR's tourists, and use the income to restart industry and reach orbit within
decades. He offers no rights and no cruelty beyond what is efficient.

**Structure.** House (a face on a screen in the Lucky 38) → Securitron police → the **Three Families**, each
a tribe he re-costumed as a casino theme: Omertas (mob, Gomorrah), Chairmen (Rat Pack, the Tops), White Glove
Society (ex-cannibal high society, Ultra-Luxe). Outside the gate, Freeside is left to the Kings (an
Elvis-impersonator gang) and the Followers.

**Technology.** Level 4, but entirely personal: one man, one bunker of robots, one chip.

**Contradictions.** Claims to be saving humanity while letting Freeside starve at his wall; his order
depends on a single 260-year-old body; each Family is plotting against him; he demands the player
exterminate the Mojave Brotherhood for purely precautionary reasons.

**Relations.** Treaty partner and parasite of NCR; despised target of the Legion; betrayed by Benny (whose
reprogrammed robot **Yes Man** provides the independent "no gods, no masters" route).

**Inspiration.** Howard Hughes (reclusive Vegas tycoon in a sealed penthouse), Randian industrialist
heroes, Old Vegas mob history.

**Critical standing.** Usually ranked with NCR as the best-argued faction: his forecast is correct, his
methods are distasteful, and the game lets you unplug him and look at what is in the pod. Gonzalez has since
questioned Yes Man, saying the route lets you finish "without getting your hands dirty".

## 6. The Master's Unity and the Children of the Cathedral

**Origin.** Richard Grey (the Fallout Bible adds that he was a Vault City exile originally named Moreau
`[SEMI-CANON]`) joined a 2102 expedition from the Hub with the trader Harold to find the source of mutant
animals. At Mariposa he fell into the FEV vats and emerged as a telepathic, absorbing mass fused to the
base computers: the Master.

**Ideology.** Humans destroyed the world because they are divided. Dip everyone in FEV and there will be one
race, immune to radiation and disease, with nothing to fight over: **the Unity**. Those unfit for conversion
may live out their lives under supervision but not breed.

**Structure.** The Master (in the vault under the Los Angeles Cathedral) → the Lieutenant (commanding
Mariposa and the dipping vats) → super mutant army with squads and patrols → **nightkin** (Stealth Boy
elites). Human front organisation: the **Children of the Cathedral**, a robed church led by the cynic
Morpheus, running hospitals in the Hub and elsewhere to scout and recruit.

**Technology.** FEV biotechnology beyond anything else alive in 2161, plus captured military hardware.

**The flaw the player can discover.** Super mutants are sterile. A Brotherhood scribe's autopsy data proves
it; shown the evidence, the Master concludes his plan is extinction with extra steps and destroys himself.
This is the founding example of a Fallout faction whose *argument can be refuted by investigation* rather
than by combat.

**Aftermath.** Defeated 2162 (Fallout Bible dating). The army scattered east and north: some became raiders,
Marcus co-founded Broken Hills, others reached the Midwest (Tactics `[SEMI-CANON]`) and eventually the
Mojave (Jacobstown, Black Mountain). See section 20.

**Inspiration.** Body-horror SF, utopian totalitarianism, cult fronts; the villain-you-can-talk-down design
came from the original team's tabletop instincts.

## 7. Followers of the Apocalypse

**Origin.** Founded in the Los Angeles Boneyard in the 2150s `[UNVERIFIED exact year]` by Nicole, a
refugee from the south who lost her parents to raiders. Based in the Boneyard library.

**Ideology.** Secular humanitarianism: teach farming, medicine and literacy for free so the mistakes that
led to the war are not repeated. Sawyer has described the New Vegas-era Followers as having a weak hierarchy
and no party line, with members ranging across anarchist and socialist positions.

**Structure.** Loose; regional administrators (Julie Farkas at the Old Mormon Fort in Freeside), doctors,
researchers. Funded by clinic fees and donations - always short of supplies.

| Game | Portrayal |
|---|---|
| FO1 | A thinly implemented good-guy group (much of their content was cut); can assault the Cathedral with the player |
| FO2 | Referenced |
| NV | A real faction with a clinic, supply quests, and a political position. They taught NCR to read and now distrust it; they schooled the Great Khans (who used the lessons to raid better) and they raised Caesar. Companion Arcade Gannon voices the creed |

**Contradiction.** Knowledge given freely empowers whoever is listening - their two greatest alumni are a
drug-running raider tribe and a slaver emperor. Refusing power means they clean up after those who take it.
The best NV endings for Freeside require them to accept somebody's protection.

**Relations.** Estranged from NCR; opposed in principle to the Brotherhood's hoarding; unofficial partners
of the Kings; ancestors of the Legion by accident.

**Inspiration.** Médecins Sans Frontières-style medical NGOs, mutual-aid anarchism, monastic teaching
orders without the monasticism.

## 8. The Institute

**Origin.** Faculty and students who survived in the basement of the Commonwealth Institute of Technology
(MIT analogue) and dug downward for two centuries. Wikis date formal founding to the early 22nd century
`[UNVERIFIED]`.

**Ideology / motto.** "Mankind - Redefined." The surface is a write-off; the future is a sealed scientific
society. Synths are equipment, not people.

**Structure.** A Director (in 2287 "Father" - the player's kidnapped son Shaun, now sixty and dying) and a
Directorate of division heads: Advanced Systems, BioScience, Robotics, Facilities, and the **Synth Retention
Bureau** (SRB), which runs informants and Courser hunters. Entry only by teleporter (the Molecular Relay).

**Technology.** Level 4: teleportation, three generations of synthetic humans (mechanical Gen 1/2,
biological Gen 3 grown from Shaun's pre-war DNA), synthetic animals, clean living. Yet it is short of
power, which is its actual plot goal (start the "Phase Three" reactor).

**Key events.** Kidnap of Shaun from Vault 111 (2227); the "Broken Mask" incident, when a synth infiltrator
massacred people in Diamond City (2229); a synth delegate murdering the representatives of the Commonwealth
Provisional Government, ending the region's one attempt at a state `[date not established]`; FEV experiments
that seeded the Commonwealth with super mutants (to 2286); the destruction of the town of University Point.

**Appearance.** Mentioned in FO3 (a side quest about a runaway android); central antagonist-or-employer of
FO4.

**Contradictions and the coherence problem.** The Institute says it wants nothing from the surface, yet
spends enormous effort abducting and replacing surface people, sabotaging surface government and dumping
failed experiments there. It builds self-aware workers and insists they are toasters while hunting the ones
who run. It hides its purpose even from the player-as-Director. Commenters on Shamus Young's long Fallout 4
critique put the usual objections precisely: the faction reads as if several writers pictured it differently
(benevolent recluses, mad scientists, despots); it has "no clear aims" beyond self-preservation; its
destruction of the Provisional Government is "never followed up". Fextralife's 2015 faction essay found
their "ambiguity made them the most boring faction of all". Defenders argue the vagueness is institutional
realism (departments pursuing projects because they can) - but the game never commits to that reading.

**Relations.** Hunted by the Brotherhood; bled by the Railroad; feared by everyone; can turn the Minutemen
into an enemy only by player action.

**Inspiration.** *Blade Runner* (replicants and retirement), *Invasion of the Body Snatchers* paranoia, and
per the official art book a *Logan's Run*-style sterile utopia.

## 9. The Railroad

**Origin.** Formed after the 2229 Broken Mask incident. Leadership passed through several hands (the
Institute smashed its headquarters in 2266) to Desdemona. Lost its Switchboard base shortly before 2287 and
now hides in the crypt of the Old North Church.

**Ideology.** Gen 3 synths are persons and slaves; free them, give them new faces and new memories, and move
them out of the Commonwealth.

**Structure.** Cell-based: HQ → agents and "heavies" (combat operatives such as Glory, herself a synth) →
safehouse keepers and runners → "tourists" (sympathisers). Tradecraft includes dead drops and chalked
**railsigns**; an Institute insider, "Patriot", feeds them escapees. A salvaged pre-war predictive AI (PAM)
does their planning.

**Technology.** Low; ingenuity, ballistic-weave clothing and a few gauss rifles.

**Appearance.** A side quest in FO3; one of four main factions in FO4; minor in Far Harbor.

**Contradictions.** They wipe the memories of the people they free - arguably killing the person to save
the body. They prioritise synths over the many enslaved or starving humans around them (an NPC defends this:
synths have "only us"). Their ending requires killing everyone in the Institute who will not evacuate and
destroying the Brotherhood. Their secrecy is theatre - the path to HQ is a tourist trail with the password
spelled on the door.

**Relations.** At war with the Institute; targeted by the Brotherhood; ignored by the Minutemen.

**Inspiration.** The Underground Railroad, Cold War spy fiction, *Blade Runner*'s sympathy for replicants.

**Critical standing.** Liked for characters (Deacon, Tinker Tom) more than for argument; often criticised as
a single-issue faction with no plan for the Commonwealth itself.

## 10. Commonwealth Minutemen

**Origin.** A volunteer militia that made its name defending Diamond City from super mutants in 2180.
Headquartered at the Castle (Fort Independence, Boston) until a giant sea creature wrecked it in 2240.
After General Becker's death (2282) the colonels feuded; at Quincy (2287) only one company answered a call
for help, a Minuteman officer defected to the Gunners, and the town was massacred. Preston Garvey leads the
last handful when the player meets him.

**Ideology.** Ordinary people protecting each other "at a minute's notice". No programme beyond that.

**Structure.** General (the player, from the first conversation), colonels, a radio station, flare guns.
Laser muskets (hand-cranked) and, later, settlement artillery.

**Role in FO4.** The faction *is* the settlement-building system plus endless radiant "a settlement needs
your help" quests. It is also the fail-safe ending: it cannot be destroyed, so a main-quest path always
remains.

**Contradictions (mostly unexplored).** The player is made supreme commander for clearing one building. The
militia already collapsed once from having no institutions, and nothing in the rebuild fixes that. The
game's backstory (Quincy, the colonels' feud) is richer than anything that happens on screen. With the
Nuka-World add-on the General can simultaneously be a raider overboss; only Preston objects.

**Relations.** Enemy of the Gunners and raiders; neutral to everyone else unless the player forces a war.

**Inspiration.** Revolutionary War minutemen and New England civic myth.

**Critical standing.** The usual verdict is "nice, and empty". Fextralife called them initially "indifferent
and, to be frank, boring" and too "squeaky clean"; Windows Central lumped them as simply "the good guys".
Insider Gaming argued (2025) they nearly match Tim Cain's wished-for "actual good faction" - but Cain's
version came with other factions *distrusting* its goodness, which is exactly the drama the Minutemen lack.

## 12. Vault City (FO2)

- **Origin:** Vault 8, a control vault, opened on schedule in 2091 and used its terraforming kit (G.E.C.K.)
  to build a clean walled town in northern Nevada.
- **Ideology:** Order, health, purity. Citizens are vault descendants (or pass a near-impossible test);
  everyone else is an outsider confined to a slum courtyard, or a "Servant" - the city bans slavery and
  practises it under another name.
- **Structure:** First Citizen (Lynette), a Council, a Proconsul who administers the citizenship test, the
  Vault City Guard. Births are scheduled; alcohol and chems are illegal.
- **Technology:** Level 3, the best medicine in the region - its main export.
- **Relations:** Despises the ghouls of Gecko, whose leaking reactor poisons its groundwater (fixing the
  reactor helps both towns; optimising it helps Vault City more); squeezed between NCR annexation pressure
  and "raiders" secretly paid by New Reno's Bishop family.
- **Contradiction:** The cleanest, safest place in the game is also its most openly bigoted, and its
  independence is doomed whichever way the player turns.
- **Inspiration:** Gated suburbia, apartheid-style pass laws, Roman titles.

## 13. The Shi (FO2)

- **Origin:** Descendants of the crew of a Chinese nuclear submarine that ran aground at San Francisco.
- **Structure:** Ruled nominally by "the Emperor" - the submarine's computer - interpreted by a chief
  adviser; guards, spies and research departments.
- **Ideology:** Scientific self-strengthening and communal survival.
- **Technology:** Level 3 and rising: working on fuel synthesis, radiation-resistant crops, armour polymers
  and a flying machine. One of the few factions doing *new* research.
- **Relations:** Cold war with the Hubologists (a Scientology parody trying to launch a spaceship); host a
  Brotherhood outpost.
- **Later:** Barely referenced afterwards; fate not established in the games.
- **Criticism:** The San Francisco area is one of Fallout 2's least-loved for tone: kung-fu film pastiche
  and accent comedy. A reminder that pop-culture reference is not characterisation.

## 14. New Reno's families (FO2)

| Family | Racket | Edge | Outside tie |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mordino | The chem Jet (invented by teenage chemist Myron, tested on slaves at "the Stables"), prostitution | Addicting the miners of Redding | Slavers |
| Salvatore | Protection, a bar | Laser pistols | Secretly trades chemicals to the Enclave |
| Bishop | Casino, mercenary "raiders" | Political reach | Covert partner of NCR against Vault City |
| Wright | Bootleg alcohol | Numbers (a huge family) | Weakest; can be steered toward temperance or power |

- **Structure:** Boss, enforcers, "made men"; the player can be made by exactly one family and is then shot
  on sight by the others' turf - simple, effective reactivity.
- **Ideology:** None; vice as an economy. The families matter because their supply chains tie the whole
  northern map together (Jet → Redding miners → gold → Vault City/NCR).
- **Inspiration:** Prohibition-era gangster films. Designed chiefly by Chris Avellone; long criticised as
  tonally too contemporary and jokey for a post-nuclear setting, and long loved for quest density.
- **Later status:** New Vegas references the Bishops and Wrights surviving `[SEMI-CANON details]`.

## 15. The Khans (all incarnations)

| Incarnation | Game / date | Base | Leader | Character |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Khans | FO1, 2161 | Camp near Shady Sands | Garl Death-Hand | One of three raider clans descended from Vault 15 (with the Vipers and Jackals). Mongol-horde styling; kidnap Tandi. Garl can be fought, bought, bluffed or impersonated |
| New Khans | FO2, 2241 | The ruins of Vault 15 | Darion, sole survivor of the first band | A revenge project against Shady Sands/NCR; keeps squatters as cover and a spy in NCR's government |
| Great Khans | NV, 2281 | Red Rock Canyon | Papa Khan; heir Regis | Driven from California, then massacred by NCR at Bitter Springs (2278), including non-combatants. Now a broken warrior tribe living on chem manufacture, allied to the Legion out of grievance |

- **Culture:** Initiation by beating, both sexes fight, honour codes, Mongol names, and (per Gonzalez)
  outlaw motorcycle club aesthetics.
- **Why they work:** They are the only raider group with a 120-year continuous history, a legitimate
  grievance and multiple futures - annihilation, absorption by the Legion, a suicidal last stand, or
  migration north to found something new with Follower help.
- **Contradiction:** Their pride requires an alliance that will erase them; they mourn Bitter Springs while
  supplying the chems that fuel the Fiends' atrocities.

## 16. Children of Atom

- **Origin:** First seen (FO3) as a small congregation in Megaton worshipping the town's unexploded bomb.
- **Theology:** Every atom contains a universe; nuclear fission ("Division") creates countless new worlds,
  so the war was a sacred act and radiation ("the Glow") a blessing.
- **By game:** FO3 - harmless eccentrics, plus a darker splinter in the Broken Steel add-on that seeks
  ghoulification. FO4 - hostile zealots with gamma guns around the Commonwealth's worst crater. Far Harbor
  add-on - a fully realised faction in a submarine base (the Nucleus) under a fanatical new High Confessor,
  holding a live missile, in a three-way standoff with fishing townsfolk and a synth refuge.
- **Structure:** Confessors, zealots, inquisitors; some members appear genuinely radiation-resistant, which
  is never fully explained `[UNVERIFIED mechanism]`.
- **Contradiction:** A death cult whose fulfilment is its own detonation; moderate members want only to be
  left alone in the fog.
- **Inspiration:** The bomb-worshipping mutants of *Beneath the Planet of the Apes* (widely noted; I found
  no developer confirmation).
- **Trajectory:** Joke → enemy type → real faction. The Far Harbor version is frequently cited as proof that
  Bethesda's writers could do faction dilemmas when given a contained space.

## Analysis A: What makes a Fallout faction compelling

| Ingredient | What it means | Strong example | Weak example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **An argument, not just an attitude** | The faction correctly diagnoses something and its remedy partly works | The Master (division causes war); House (he predicted the war and saved a city); NCR (law beats warlords) | Talon Company; East Coast super mutants |
| **Visible material basis** | You can see what they eat, who pays, how goods move, what they are short of | NCR's ranchers, currency, conscripts, water deals; New Reno's supply chains; Vault City's medicine exports | Institute (no economy shown, wants nothing nameable); Minutemen (no taxes, no supply, no politics) |
| **Discoverable flaw** | The decisive weakness is found by exploring, and can be used in dialogue | Mutant sterility; Caesar's tumour and Auto-Doc; Danse's identity; Hanlon's falsified reports | Railroad's memory wipes (noted, never consequential) |
| **Internal disagreement** | Members contest the faction's direction | Lyons vs. Outcasts; McNamara vs. Hardin; Oliver vs. Hanlon; Rahmani vs. Shin; Eden vs. Autumn | Minutemen (nobody disagrees with anything) |
| **Reactivity** | The faction notices and remembers what you do, and has several end states | NV reputation system and ending slides; FO2 town endings; Khans' four fates | FO3 (factions are fixed roles); FO4 radiant quests |
| **Proportionate player role** | You are an agent, ally or fixer - not instantly the boss | NV (courier doing favours for powers greater than you) | FO4 (General in an hour; Director of the Institute by inheritance) |
| **A cost to joining** | Siding with them closes doors and dirties your hands | FO4's forced betrayals, at least structurally; New Reno's made-man system | Yes Man route (by its writer's own account) |

Sawyer's formulation, reported by PC Gamer, is that NCR and the Legion feel real because their members
disagree and challenge each other: you "have to wrestle with the fact that it contains multitudes". The
practical corollary is that every faction needs at least one member who thinks it is wrong, one who thinks
it does not go far enough, and one who is just there for the pay.

and noted as such.

| Faction | Usual verdict | Why |

any route to reform a faction from the top despite the player holding its top job.

## Analysis C: Post-apocalyptic vs. post-post-apocalyptic

**The positions.**

- **Rebuild (Interplay / Obsidian line).** Fallout 1 already has towns, trade companies and a water
  economy 84 years after the war; Fallout 2 adds a nation-state; New Vegas adds two rival states, a
  currency war and a treaty system. Sawyer told the Guardian the game is "in some ways, a
  post-post-apocalyptic story". The apocalypse is the *premise*; the subject is what people build next and
  whether they repeat the old mistakes ("war never changes" as a claim about politics, not rubble).
- **Ruins (Bethesda line).** Fallout 3 (200 years on) and Fallout 4 (210) present skeletons still in beds,
  unlooted supermarkets and settlements made of scrap. Factions are local bands, not states. The appeal is
  exploration, scarcity and the retro-future aesthetic; a functioning republic would spoil the mood and the
  loot loop. Critics such as Shamus Young mock this as two centuries in which "nothing has happened"; a
  commenter there put the objection well: the setting is supposed to be transitional, but "it never actually
  changes".
- **Reset (TV series).** The show destroyed the NCR's capital off-screen. Its showrunners argue the
  opposite of stasis - that a wasteland which "stays as it is decade-to-decade is preposterous" - but the
  effect is to return the West Coast to frontier conditions. Sawyer has said publicly he does not mind.
- **Before the fall (76).** Solves the problem by going earliest: 25 years after the bombs, ruin is
  plausible. The cost was a world with no one alive in it at launch.

**What the debate is really about.**

1. *Franchise maintenance vs. consequence.* A setting that rebuilds eventually stops being the product
   advertised on the box. A setting that never rebuilds makes every faction's project pointless and every
   player victory temporary.
2. *Scale.* NCR at 700,000 citizens is hard to depict and hard for one protagonist to matter to. Small
   regional factions keep the player central. New Vegas's solution - set the game on the big state's
   overstretched frontier - gets both.
3. *Plausibility of timescale.* Much of the criticism of the Bethesda games disappears if the date is moved
   from 200 years to 20. The date, not the tone, is the problem.
4. *Tone.* Some players simply prefer the bleak scavenger fantasy; New Vegas is regularly described as more
   Western than apocalypse.

## Design Takeaways

- **Write the argument first.** For each faction, write the one-paragraph case a smart member would make,
  and the one fact that most damages it. If we cannot write both, the faction is not ready.
- **Show the economy.** Every faction gets an answer on screen to: what do they eat, what do they sell,
  what are they short of, who do they owe? Make at least one quest per faction about logistics.
- **Deliver the faction's best claim in play, not in dialogue.** The Legion's lesson: if a faction's selling
  point is "our roads are safe", the player must walk a safe road. Budget for the home territory, not just
  the front line - or do not make the claim.
- **Three dissenters per faction** (too soft / too hard / just here for pay), each quest-capable. Schisms are
  also our cheapest tool for regional variety - the Brotherhood trick - but use it once, not six times.
- **Hide a discoverable flaw** that changes available endings and can be raised in conversation with the
  leader. Reward investigation with leverage, not only lore.
- **Keep the player's rank proportionate.** Agent, envoy, fixer, kingmaker - not instant commander. If the
  player can lead, leadership must come with policy choices that change the faction.
- **Avoid a costless good option.** If we include a mutual-aid faction, take Cain's suggestion: make its
  goodness itself contested (others suspect it; it is naive about who uses its help - the Followers'
  Caesar problem). And if we include an independent route, it must dirty the player's hands.
- **Do not build a faction around a mystery we cannot pay off.** The Institute's lesson: decide the goal,
  then decide what to conceal. Secrecy without an answer reads as incoherence.
- **No mandatory genocide endings.** Allow truce, absorption, coup, exile and reform outcomes; make total
  destruction one option with a price.
- **No goal-less armed groups.** Every hostile human group needs a name, a reason, a food source and at
  least one member who will talk. A mercenary company must be hireable and bribable.
- **Mutated or marginal peoples are societies first.** Give them towns, disagreements and a demographic
  problem (sterility was a gift to the writers). Never rely on "one good one" as characterisation.
- **Pick our date deliberately and let time pass.** Decide how many years after the collapse we are and
  make ruins, institutions and scarcity consistent with it. Prefer the *frontier of a rebuilt power*: it
  gives large-scale politics, a small-scale map, and a reason for the player to matter.
- **Dead factions are seasoning, not the meal.** Posthumous logs make good tragedy; use them for one
  faction whose failure teaches the player something about the living ones.
- **Reference with restraint.** New Reno and the Shi show how quickly pastiche dates and how easily it
  turns into stereotype. Our factions should be inspired by history and political ideas, not by films.
- **Originality check.** Do not reproduce the recognisable packages: power-armoured tech monks, a
  Roman-costumed slaver army, a hidden android-building academy, a bomb-worshipping church. Take the
  mechanisms (schism, discoverable flaw, material basis), not the costumes.

## Gaps and confidence notes

- Sawyer's exact statements about cut Legion territory and Gonzalez's full remarks on Yes Man were reached
  only as headlines or secondary quotations; treat wording as approximate.
- The Fallout Bible itself could not be fetched (host blocked); Bible-derived facts (2186 founding, Grey's
  earlier name, 2162 dating, 700,000 population) come from memory of wiki summaries and are flagged.
- Nukapedia (fallout.fandom.com) blocked automated access; fallout.wiki and Wikipedia were the wiki sources
  actually read. Several wiki pages were long enough that only their first portion was read.
- Dates for the Institute's founding, the Provisional Government massacre, and the Followers' founding are
  not firmly established here.
- New Reno and Vault City's post-Fallout 2 status rests on semi-canon material.
- The Shi criticism (orientalist pastiche) and the "orcs" characterisation of East Coast mutants are
  long-standing community readings; I did not locate a single authoritative published essay for either.
