# Fallout (1997, Interplay) — Lore and Canon Reference

Scope: the story, world, factions, characters, endings, and canon status of the first Fallout game.
Mechanics are covered only where they shape the narrative (time limits, skill-gated endings).
Confidence tags follow `research/CONVENTIONS.md`. Where a detail comes from the author's general knowledge of
the game and was not re-checked against a source during this research pass, it is tagged `[UNVERIFIED]`.

## Quick Reference

- **Released** 10 October 1997 by Interplay (team later became Black Isle). Lead: Tim Cain; lead designer:
  Chris Taylor; art director: Leonard Boyarsky. Roughly 3.5 years and about $3 million to make.
- **Setting:** Southern/central California, starting **5 December 2161**, 84 years after a two-hour nuclear
  war on 23 October 2077. Canonical story ends 10 May 2162.
- **Premise:** an underground shelter's water-purification chip fails; one resident is sent out alone with
  **150 days** to find a replacement. Solving that reveals a much larger threat.
- **Structure:** two-act plot. Act 1 is a personal, timed fetch quest (water chip). Act 2 is an open
  investigation ending in two targets (the mutant leader and the mutant factory) that can be done in either order.
- **Antagonist:** the Master, a fused bio-mass of people and computer hardware who wants to end human
  conflict by converting everyone into one sterile-but-superior mutant species ("the Unity").
- **Signature moment:** the final boss can be talked into suicide by proving his mutants are infertile.
  Three other resolutions exist (fight, sneak-and-nuke, join him).
- **Map:** about 12 major locations; each town is a self-contained moral vignette with its own ending slide.
- **Ending:** a slideshow of per-settlement outcomes, then the hero is exiled from home by the leader who
  sent them. The "reward" for saving everyone is banishment.
- **Hidden clocks:** mutants overrun towns on fixed days (Necropolis day 110, Hub 140, Brotherhood 170,
  Junktown 210, Shady Sands 230, Vault 13 day 500 in v1.0; patched to 13 years in v1.1).
- **Companions:** four (Ian, Dogmeat, Tycho, Katja), thinly written, uncontrollable; two die in canon.
- **Tone:** bleak, sardonic, 1950s-atomic-age optimism laid over ruin; violence is ugly and funny at once.
- **Core themes:** "war never changes"; utopias built by coercion; isolation versus engagement; scarcity;
  the cost of being the one who goes outside.
- **Cut content matters:** several "good" endings (Hub, Followers, Necropolis survival) are unreachable or
  bugged in the shipped game; Junktown's endings were inverted late by marketing.
- **Canon (per Fallout 2 and the Fallout Bible):** water chip found in Necropolis, Ian and Dogmeat die,
  Master and vats both destroyed, hero exiled, founds the tribal village of Arroyo, vanishes in 2208.
- **Biggest later retcons:** vaults as social experiments; the Master's pre-mutation biography; whether
  ghouls come from radiation or virus; the Brotherhood's origin story being extended; super mutants no
  longer unique to the Master.

## 1. Setting

### 1.1 World state in 2161

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| Divergence | An alternate Earth where the 1950s vision of the future came true (atomics, robots, vacuum tubes) and culture froze; resource wars lead to nuclear exchange between the US and China. |
| The Great War | 23 October 2077; lasted about two hours. |
| Start of game | 5 December 2161 (84 years later). Late enough that pre-war survivors are almost all dead, early enough that scavenged goods still exist. |
| Region | Southern and central California: from the Sierra Nevada (Vault 13) south to the ruins of Los Angeles (Boneyard) and near San Diego (the Glow). |
| Society | Scattered small settlements (a few dozen to about 1,000 people), no states, no long-range government. Trade runs by caravan out of one merchant city. |
| Currency | Bottle caps, explicitly backed by the Hub's merchants and their control of water. |
| Technology | Pre-war tech is a scavenged, poorly understood resource. One faction hoards it. |
| Threats | Raiders, mutated animals (radscorpions, giant rats, the near-mythical deathclaw), radiation, and a growing army of super mutants. |
| Mutation sources | Radiation and the Forced Evolutionary Virus (FEV), a pre-war military bio-project. |

## 2. Protagonist and Premise

- **The Vault Dweller**: a player-defined resident of Vault 13 (three pre-made characters are offered:
  Albert the diplomat, Natalia the thief, Max Stone the bruiser). No fixed gender, name, or personality in
  the game itself. The Fallout Bible treats the canonical Dweller as male `[SEMI-CANON]`.
- **The hook** is delivered by the Vault's Overseer in a single short briefing: the water chip is dead, there
  is no spare, the Vault has 150 days, try Vault 15 to the east. The player is given a pistol, a knife, and
  a canteen-level sendoff. The first thing seen outside the door is the skeleton of the previous person
  sent (Ed).
- **Why it works:** the goal is concrete, small, and urgent; the player knows nothing about the outside,
  exactly like the character; every town is initially just a lead on a spare part.

## 3. Main Plot, Beat by Beat

### Act 1 — The Water Chip

1. **Vault 13.** Briefing; exit through a rat-infested cave.
2. **Shady Sands** (on the road to Vault 15). A farming village. Optional help with radscorpions; meet Ian;
   learn about raiders, Junktown, and the Hub. First demonstration that problems have several solutions.
3. **Vault 15.** A collapsed, looted ruin. The control room is buried under rock: no chip. The only lead
   dies here, forcing the player to explore. (A rope is needed to descend the elevator shafts.)
4. **Junktown and the Hub.** Information-gathering. At the Hub the Water Merchants will sell a water
   caravan to Vault 13 (extends the deadline by 100 days) and mention that Necropolis must have working
   water because it never buys any. Hiring them also leaks the Vault's location, quietly shortening the
   hidden mutant-invasion clock.
5. **Necropolis.** A city of ghouls occupied by a super mutant garrison at the waterworks. The chip is in
   the ghoul-infested Vault 12 beneath the city. Taking it dooms the ghouls to thirst unless the player
   also repairs their backup pump with scavenged parts.
6. **Return to Vault 13.** The Overseer installs the chip, files the report, and notices the report's
   content: the mutants are too numerous and too uniform to be natural. New orders: find and destroy the
   source, and its leader.

### Act 2 — The Mutant Threat

Leads are spread across the map and can be assembled in any order:

| Lead | Where | What it reveals |
|---|---|---|
| Missing caravans | The Hub (Far Go Traders, then Harold, then a deathclaw lair with a dying mutant's recording) | Caravans are being taken by mutants, not a monster. |
| Harold's story | The Hub, Old Town | The 2102 expedition; Richard Grey; a military base in the north-west. |
| Brotherhood archives and Vree's autopsy | Lost Hills | Super mutants are sterile; the Brotherhood suspects a base to the north. |
| West Tek records / ZAX | The Glow | What FEV is, and that the project moved to Mariposa. |
| Children of the Cathedral | Hub hospital, Boneyard, the Followers of the Apocalypse | The friendly church is a front run by the mutants' leader. |
| Captured and interrogated | Mutant patrols, Necropolis | The Lieutenant personally explains the plan (at the cost of probable death). |

Two targets, either order:

7. **The Cathedral** (south of the Boneyard). Infiltrate the cult (badges, robes, or force), descend into
   the demonstration vault beneath it, pass a corridor of living flesh that attacks the mind, and confront
   the Master. Resolve by argument, combat, or by arming the nuclear device on the lowest level.
8. **Mariposa Military Base** (far north-west). Get past the gate (bluff, disguise, radio trick, or
   assault, optionally with Brotherhood paladins), defeat or bypass the Lieutenant, and trigger the base's
   self-destruct, destroying the FEV vats.
9. **Return to Vault 13.** The Overseer meets the hero outside the door, thanks them, and exiles them:
   the hero has changed, and young people would follow them out and empty the Vault. The Dweller walks
   into the desert alone. (An evil or "Bloody Mess" character shoots the Overseer first.)

### Fail states

- Water runs out (day 150, or 250 with the caravan): cinematic of the Vault's dead.
- Mutant invasion clock expires, or the player reveals the Vault's location under interrogation, or joins
  the Master: cinematic of super mutants massacring Vault 13 (and, on the "join" path, the Dweller being
  dipped).

### 4.2 Location notes

**Vault 13.** A handful of named residents (Theresa and Lyle lead a "we should leave" faction; a water
thief is stealing rations). The Overseer is not a villain: he is frightened, dutiful, and willing to spend
one life to save the rest. The Vault's music and sterile corridors establish what the hero is fighting to
preserve, which makes the exile land.

**Shady Sands.** Founded by Vault 15 emigrants. Aradesh speaks in homilies and distrusts outsiders;
Tandi is bored and wants to leave. Quests: destroy the radscorpion nest (kill them or collapse the cave
with explosives), cure a poisoned villager with antidote made from scorpion tails, advise on crop
rotation, rescue Tandi.

**Khan camp.** Tandi's rescue can be resolved by: paying ransom, winning an unarmed duel with Garl,
killing everyone, sneaking her out by lockpicking, or being mistaken for Garl's dead father (whom he
murdered) and frightening him into compliance `[UNVERIFIED]` for the exact trigger. The player can also
join the Khans by murdering a captive. Two other clans (Vipers, Jackals) are named but were cut as locations.

**Junktown.** Three zones: gate (guns holstered; gate shuts at night), Killian's store and the hotel,
Gizmo's casino. Entering Killian's shop triggers an assassination attempt. Killian then asks the player
to get Gizmo's confession (wear a wire or plant a bug); Gizmo offers to pay for Killian's death instead.
Side stories: the Skulz gang's protection racket on the bar, a hostage situation in the hotel, a boxer
and his girlfriend, a town doctor who quietly sells human meat to a food stall in the Hub.

**The Hub.** Districts: entrance/farms, Downtown (police, caravan houses, loan shark, bar), Old Town
(slum: Harold, thieves' guild, a captive Brotherhood initiate), Water Merchants, the Heights (rich).
Three caravan houses (Water Merchants, Crimson Caravan, Far Go Traders) hire the player as a guard. The
crime boss Decker offers two assassinations (a merchant and his wife; the cult's priestess), which can be
reported to the sheriff for a raid on Decker instead.

**Necropolis.** Set is theatrical, hostile, and wants the mutants dead. The sewer ghouls are gentle and
only want water. The mutant garrison leader can be bluffed, fought, or allowed to capture the player.
On day 110 the mutants massacre the city regardless of how well the player treated it.

**Lost Hills.** Entry requires an "initiation": fetch proof from the Glow, a quest the gate guards give
as a joke because they expect the applicant to die. Inside: training, implants, power armour to repair,
Head Scribe Vree's mutant autopsy (the key to the Master argument), High Elder John Maxson's scouting
mission north. Elders debate whether to intervene; the player must persuade them.

**The Glow.** A six-level crater facility; entering without anti-radiation drugs is fatal. The player
restores power, meets ZAX (a chess-playing mainframe that calmly explains FEV), and recovers the
research logs and the dead Brotherhood expedition's record. Almost no combat-free NPC interaction: the
story is told by logs and by an AI. A model for environmental storytelling.

**Boneyard.** Adytum's mayor, Jon Zimmerman, hires the player to kill Razor, leader of the Blades gang,
for murdering his son. Razor has a recording proving the town's own hired guards, the Regulators, did it.
The player can side with the Regulators (easy money) or arm the Blades by clearing a deathclaw nest for
the Gun Runners, then free Adytum. The Followers of the Apocalypse, pacifist scholars in the old library
led by Nicole, know the Cathedral is a fraud and supply a contact inside it.

**The Cathedral.** A working church with sermons, a gift shop, and a clinic on top; a tower of nightkin
and the high priest Morpheus; beneath, a pre-war demonstration vault holding mutant technicians, captive
psychics the Master experiments on, the Master's flesh-grown throne room, and a nuclear device.

**Mariposa.** Four levels (two "stronghold", two "vats"), force-field corridors, robots that can be
reprogrammed, and the Lieutenant, an articulate, sadistic true believer. The self-destruct can be set
from the vat control computer or forced with explosives.

## 7. The Antagonist: The Master and the Unity

### 7.1 Biography

- Born Richard Moreau; exiled from his home vault for murder; renamed himself Richard Grey; became a
  doctor in the Hub (`[RETCON]`: the Vault 8 / Moreau origin was added by Fallout 2 and the Fallout Bible;
  in Fallout 1 he is simply Richard Grey, a Hub physician).
- 2102: leads an expedition with Harold to find the source of mutated animals; at Mariposa a robotic arm
  knocks him into an FEV vat. He crawls out weeks later, mutating, in agony, and recording an audio diary.
- He absorbs animals, then people, then the base's computer systems into his own mass. Intelligence and
  psychic ability grow with each mind consumed; so does fragmentation.
- He concludes that the war happened because humans are divided and weak, and that FEV is the cure: one
  race, one purpose. He dips captives. Irradiated wastelanders mostly produce dull brutes; "prime normals"
  (unirradiated vault people) produce the intelligent mutants he needs. Hence the hunt for vaults.
- By the 2150s he has moved under Los Angeles and adopted a cult as his human-facing church.

### 7.2 The plan

Convert every suitable human; forbid the unsuitable from reproducing, letting them live out their lives
in peace under mutant protection; in one generation there is one species and no more war. The Lieutenant
describes the mutants as the next step of evolution; the Master frames it as mercy.

### 7.3 Presentation

- A body fused into a command chair and corridor of flesh, with a monitor for one eye.
- Speech switches mid-sentence between a calm male voice, an enraged male voice, a female voice, and a
  synthetic voice stitching words together. The effect dramatises a mind made of many people who do not
  fully agree. Developer accounts say the idea came from asking what would happen if more than one person
  went into the vat, and that the design was "a man, a woman and a computer terminal" fused.
- He is polite. He offers the player a place. He argues.

### 7.4 Ways to resolve the confrontation

| Method | Requirements | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| Persuasion | Vree's autopsy data (or equivalent knowledge), adequate Intelligence (about 7) and a strong Speech skill | The player shows that super mutants are sterile: the Unity dies out in one generation. The Master checks with his own female mutants, realises decades of atrocity were for nothing, and starts the self-destruct. "I don't think that I can continue." |
| Combat | A powerful late-game build | He has roughly 500 HP, a gatling laser, and summons mutants through side doors. Death triggers the countdown. |
| Stealth and sabotage | Lockpick/Science/Traps around 70%, or a key obtained at Mariposa | Skip him entirely; arm the nuclear device on the level below and run. |
| Join the Unity | Agree, or give up Vault 13's location | Bad ending: Vault 13 is taken and the Dweller is dipped. |

Approach also varies: cult robes let the player walk past mutants; a psychic-nullifier device taken from
one of the captive psychics negates the mental damage in the flesh corridor `[UNVERIFIED]` on exact source
of the device.

### 7.5 Why he is considered well written

1. **He has a coherent answer to the game's opening line.** If war never changes because people never
   change, then change people. The villain engages the theme rather than merely opposing the hero.
2. **He believes he is the hero.** Designer Scott Bennie described him as a well-intentioned figure; the
   team's stated aim was to hit the player with emotional weight, not a monster to shoot.
3. **He is beaten by evidence, not by a slogan.** The Speech victory is the payoff of an investigation
   (autopsy, archives), so it feels earned; and he verifies the claim before accepting it.
4. **He has the integrity to lose.** Presented with proof, he does not rage; he gives up and ends it.
   This retroactively makes him tragic.
5. **Form matches content.** The fractured voices are a literal "unity" that is not unified.
6. **His lieutenants bracket him.** Morpheus (cynic) and the Lieutenant (zealot) show the belief decaying
   outward from its source.
7. **The player's build determines the story.** Critics (IGN, Kotaku, GameSpot, GamesRadar, per the
   Wikipedia reception summary) singled out the talk-down as proof that dialogue could be as valid as
   combat; GameSpot called him "one of the most memorable antagonists in computer-gaming history."

Known weakness: fans regularly note that a genius who dissects people for a living should have noticed
sterility in 30-plus years. Defenders answer that he is insane, surrounded by yes-men, and that mutants
are near-immortal so nobody was counting births. `[DISPUTED]` as a plot hole.

## 8. Notable Side Quests and Their Moral Structure

| Quest | Location | Shape of the choice | What it teaches |
|---|---|---|---|
| Radscorpion nest | Shady Sands | Kill them or seal the cave | Skills other than combat solve problems |
| Rescue Tandi | Khan camp | Pay, duel, sneak, slaughter, bluff | One objective, five verbs; early statement of the design philosophy |
| Killian versus Gizmo | Junktown | Law or vice; inform or assassinate | The lawful side is not obviously the better ruler (see cut endings) |
| Bust or join the Skulz | Junktown | Infiltrate and inform, or commit the crime | Undercover work means doing something wrong first |
| Hotel hostage | Junktown | Talk the gunman down or kill him | Better reward for the peaceful resolution |
| Doc Morbid / Iguana Bob | Junktown to Hub | Discover the human-meat trade; blackmail or (cut) report | Evil that is profitable to leave in place; the "report him" path was cut |
| Decker's contracts | The Hub | Do the murders, or turn informant | Crime pays more cash; law pays more experience |
| Missing caravans | The Hub | Pure investigation | The folk explanation (a monster) is wrong; follow evidence |
| Loxley's heist | The Hub | Steal from the richest man in town | Low-stakes charm; thieves with a code |
| Irwin's farm | The Hub | Clear squatters for a farmer | Small good deed, unique weapon |
| The Glow initiation | Lost Hills | Do the impossible errand | A joke quest that becomes the lore dump of the game |
| Watershed and water pump | Necropolis | Take the chip and leave, or fix their pump first | The main quest itself harms innocents unless the player does extra, unrewarded work |
| Adytum: Razor or the Regulators | Boneyard | Believe the client or the target | The quest-giver is being lied to; the "gang" is the victim |
| Deathclaw nest | Boneyard | Destroy the mother and eggs | Required for the good Adytum path; monster-as-ecology |
| Followers' spy | Boneyard | Find a cult infiltrator | Largely cut; still referenced by the ending logic |

General moral design:

- **No morality meter choices in dialogue.** A single karma number plus a few reputation titles
  (Champion, Berserker, Childkiller) track behaviour; consequences are mostly local and in the epilogue.
- **Evil is usually the better-paid, lower-experience path**; good is slower and often requires the right
  skill.
- **Several quests hide the truth from the quest-giver**, so obedience is not virtue.
- **Good outcomes can be undone by time.** A town the player saved can still be massacred if the player
  dawdles. Heroism is necessary but not sufficient.

## 10. Themes and Tone

| Theme | How the game expresses it |
|---|---|
| "War never changes" | Opening narration; every faction reproduces a pre-war failure (monopoly, militarism, cult, police state). |
| Coerced utopia | The Master's Unity: peace by removing choice and difference. |
| Isolation versus engagement | Vault 13 and the Brotherhood both hide; both are saved only by someone who leaves; both resist that person afterwards. |
| The cost of the hero | Exile. Experience of the outside makes the Dweller a contaminant. |
| Scarcity and time | Water as currency, plot driver, and literal countdown. |
| The guilt of the old world | The Glow and Mariposa: the monsters are government projects. |
| Prejudice and the mutated | Ghouls and Harold are sympathetic; "pure" humans are often the worst people present. |
| Unreliable authority | Zimmerman, the Overseer, Killian, Brotherhood elders: quest-givers are wrong, deceived, or self-interested. |

**Tone.** Grim material delivered deadpan. The interface mascot grins through descriptions of
dismemberment; item text is sarcastic; death animations are grotesque to the point of comedy. Art is
brown, industrial, and lonely; the ambient score (Mark Morgan) is drones and metal, not melody. Humour is
mostly dry and in-world, with a few fourth-wall "special encounters" (a crashed saucer, a police box, a
giant footprint) gated behind the Luck stat so most players see few of them.

**Influences** cited by developers and commentators: Wasteland (1988), Mad Max 2, A Boy and His Dog,
Forbidden Planet, 1950s civil-defence media, and the tabletop GURPS rules the game was built on until
the licence was dropped in early 1997.

## Design Takeaways

- **Open with a small, physical, urgent need.** "Our water machine broke" beats "save the world". Let the
  world-scale threat be discovered as a consequence of solving the small problem.
- **Make the second act an investigation with redundant leads.** Six different places point at the
  villain; missing three of them does not block progress. Copy the redundancy.
- **Give the antagonist an answer to the game's thesis, and let the player disprove it with evidence
  gathered during play.** The talk-down works because it is the reward for curiosity, not for a high
  number alone. Gate it on knowledge the player had to find.
- **Build the villain's form out of his idea.** Many voices in one body; a "unity" that is visibly not
  one. Avoid a villain whose look is merely scary.
- **Bracket the true believer with a cynic and a zealot** so the ideology has a texture, not a single face.
- **One problem, many verbs.** The hostage rescue with five solutions in the second town teaches the
  whole game. Put our clearest multi-solution quest very early.
- **Towns as moral vignettes with an epilogue slide each** is cheap and enormously effective. But make
  towns react during play as well; Fallout 1 saves nearly all consequence for the slideshow.
- **Let the quest-giver be wrong.** The Adytum reversal (the client's guards are the killers) is the
  model: reward players who talk to the target before pulling the trigger.
- **Be careful with invisible clocks.** The water timer creates real tension and is fondly remembered;
  the hidden town-destruction timers punish exploration, were softened by patch, and still draw
  complaints. If we use deadlines, show them, and make them movable by player action.
- **Do not ship unreachable good endings.** Bugs made the game harsher than intended and players noticed.
  Every epilogue branch needs an automated test.
- **A bittersweet personal ending is memorable; a fixed one costs agency.** Consider letting the world
  outcome vary widely while the personal cost varies in kind, not in existence.
- **Do not let marketing flatten moral ambiguity.** The original Junktown design (orderly tyrant versus
  prosperous crook) was the more interesting statement. Protect at least one such inversion.
- **Companions were the weakest element:** no control, no arcs, friendly fire. If we have companions,
  they need opinions, stakes in the plot, and commands.
- **Tell backstory through places and machines** (the Glow, the audio diary) rather than narrators. A
  polite computer explaining an atrocity is more chilling than a cutscene.
- **Avoid lore that later needs patching.** The series spent decades retconning vault purposes, mutation
  causes, and faction origins. Write our own setting bible before the first game ships, including what
  is deliberately left unknown.
- **Do not reuse:** vaults with numbered jumpsuits, bottle-cap currency, a forced-evolution virus, power-
  armoured tech monks, or a multi-voiced fused overlord. Take the structures, not the nouns.
