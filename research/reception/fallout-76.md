# Reception: Fallout 76 (2018 - present)

Scope: critical and player reception of Bethesda Game Studios' online *Fallout 76* from its 14 November 2018
launch to November 2026: launch scores, the full controversy list, reported development problems, the
recovery through *Wastelanders* and later updates, re-review scores, Steam review trend, what current
players like and dislike, and player counts including both TV-show spikes.

Research note: IGN, Eurogamer, GameSpot, The Guardian, Polygon, Rock Paper Shotgun, Business Insider, Ars
Technica and Reddit blocked direct fetching, and the session's web-search budget ran out part-way through.
Claims that rest on a secondary summary (Wikipedia, OpenCritic/Metacritic listings, search digests, a
third-party digest of a video) or on the researcher's memory are flagged. See "Gaps".

## Quick Reference

- Launch Metascores: **52 (PC), 53 (PS4), 49 (Xbox One)**. OpenCritic: **54 average, 9% of critics
  recommend, "Weak" tier** (150 reviews, includes later re-reviews). Lowest-rated mainline entry by about
  30 points.
- Metacritic user scores are still **2.9 (PC), 2.7 (PS4), 3.0 (Xbox One)** in 2026, roughly 70-73% negative.
  Launch-era anger is permanently recorded there.
- Launch verdicts in a phrase each: Eurogamer "Avoid"; GameSpot 4/10 "soulless"; Guardian 2/5 "pointless
  walk"; IGN 5/10; PC Gamer 60; Game Informer 6/10 "wasn't ready for showtime".
- Three separate failures stacked: (1) technical state, (2) a design with no human NPCs, dialogue or
  consequence, (3) a string of customer-trust incidents outside the game itself.
- Trust incidents within the first seven weeks: nylon bag sold as canvas, 500-Atom (US$5) compensation
  offer, support-site leak of bag claimants' personal data, refund refusals, $80 rum in a plastic shell,
  essay-for-unban emails, premium holiday cosmetics, and a January patch that reintroduced fixed bugs.
- Price fell from $60 to roughly $35-40 within about a week (per Internet Historian's account).
- SuperData estimated **1.4 million digital copies by end of 2018**; physical sales reportedly down about
  82% versus *Fallout 4*.
- Fallout 1st (23 October 2019): $12.99/month or $99.99/year for private servers and an unlimited scrap
  box. It launched broken (non-private "private" worlds, scrap deleted) at the game's reputational low.
- Kotaku's June 2022 investigation (10 former staff): mandatory QA crunch of 10-hour days, six days a week;
  engine unsuited to persistent online worlds; multiplayer expertise sidelined; most designers wanted NPCs
  and were overruled; "every major launch bug was known to QA".
- Todd Howard, June 2019: "It's not how you launch, it's what it becomes." Also conceded the team never
  expected a high Metacritic score.
- *Wastelanders* (free, 14 April 2020) added human NPCs, dialogue trees with skill checks, two rival
  factions and instanced story spaces. Metascore **69 PC / 63 PS4**. IGN 6/10.
- Steam launch day (same date) was review-bombed to 34% positive (one outlet saw under 20% briefly),
  recovered to 67% within two days and 79% within a month.
- IGN re-reviewed the whole game in May 2024: **7/10** (was 5/10).
- Player milestones: 11 million (Nov 2021), 17 million (Dec 2023 / Mar 2024), **1 million in a single day**
  after the TV show (April 2024). Steam all-time peak about **73,000** (April 2024), versus a 6,000-9,000
  monthly average baseline.
- Steam sentiment has stalled and is now sliding: about 78% positive in 2020, 73-74% in 2021-2024, **68% in
  2025, 65% in 2026 to date, 63% in the last 30 days** (histogram endpoint, read 9 November 2026).
- What works for current players: Appalachia as a map, co-op exploration, C.A.M.P. building and showing
  off, public events, a notably welcoming community.
- What still does not: monetisation layering (box price + subscription + cash shop + season pass), stash
  and inventory friction eased by the subscription, bugs returning with patches, endgame grind, story
  choices that cannot change a shared world.
- Net: the game was commercially rescued and is alive eight years on, but it never became well-reviewed,
  and "Fallout 76" remains shorthand for a botched launch.

## Reported Development Problems

Sources: Jason Schreier's May 2018 Kotaku scoop (three sources) and Sisi Jiang's June 2022 Kotaku
investigation (10 former Bethesda/ZeniMax staff; Bethesda did not comment), plus trade summaries.

| Area | Reported problem |
|---|---|
| Origin | Began as a multiplayer prototype for *Fallout 4*; grew into an online survival RPG "heavily inspired" by *DayZ* and *Rust*, with design changing rapidly (Schreier, 2018). Wikipedia dates the multiplayer work to 2013, with a greenlight after *Fallout 4* shipped in November 2015. |
| Studios | Split between Rockville (Maryland) and Austin, the former BattleCry Studios whose own online game had been cancelled. Austin adapted old id Software netcode to the engine (Wikipedia). |
| Engine | Creation Engine assumes one player and only simulates what is near that player. Persistent shared worlds fought that assumption at every level. Tools were fragile: updating a build could break it. |
| Expertise ignored | Experienced multiplayer designers in both offices, and the publisher's MMO studio, were "routinely sidelined" until after launch. Designers lacked basics such as expected players per server. |
| Direction | Sources describe no coherent vision across about three years; the executive producer was mostly on the next single-player project and dropped in with feedback ("seagulling"); the design director reportedly disengaged. |
| No-NPC decision | Most designers wanted human NPCs. Leadership refused before launch; schedule pressure from the multiplayer conversion then made it moot. |
| Warnings dismissed | Griefing, stability and quest checkpointing concerns were raised and postponed. |
| QA crunch | 10-hour days, six days a week before the beta; some staff cite 10-16 hour days. Weekend overtime was "voluntold". About $200 and pizza per weekend. One weekend was spent testing a build that did not contain the fix they were called in to verify. |
| QA process | About three days per week to test the whole game, one day if a build was late. Testers could not talk directly to the developers fixing bugs. Breaks were timed and monitored. |
| Known issues shipped | "Every major launch bug was known to QA beforehand" (paraphrase of a developer source). |
| Attrition | Veteran staff of 20 years left; QA headcount fell continuously; staff were pulled from two other in-development games to support the live service. "It ate people." |
| After launch | Testers moderating player reports received abuse and death threats. |
| Leadership's public position | Howard (June 2019): the criticism was anticipated, it was never going to be "a high Metacritic game", and "it's not how you launch, it's what it becomes". Later interviews conceded a longer public beta would have helped (GameSpot, Oct 2019, via Wikipedia's citation). |

## What Current Players Like

Direct sampling of Reddit and Steam review text was blocked; this section synthesises critic re-reviews,
launch reviews' positives that have held, the update record and (flagged) general knowledge of the community.

| Like | Detail | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Appalachia | Six visually distinct regions, vertical terrain, regional folklore monsters, strong location-level storytelling. Praised even at launch and never revised downward. | IGN 2018, PC Gamer 2018, PCWorld 2018/2020 |

## Lessons: Launch Quality, Trust, Long-Tail Reputation

- **Launch scores are permanent.** Eight years of work moved critics from about 52 to at best 7/10, and
  the original Metascores and 2.7-3.0 user scores still stand as the first thing anyone sees.
- **You can recover revenue and a player base without recovering reputation.** The game is alive, has a
  loyal population and spikes with each TV season, yet remains a punchline. Those are separate ledgers.
- **Trust failures compound faster than product failures.** A buggy launch is forgivable. A buggy launch
  followed by a cheapened collector's item, a $5 apology, a data leak caused by the apology process, refused
  refunds and premium cosmetics reads as contempt. Each incident on its own was small.
- **Monetisation is judged against goodwill balance.** Fallout 1st might have been tolerated on a healthy
  game. Launched at the lowest point, selling fixes for self-inflicted friction, and itself broken, it became
  the emblem of the whole affair.
- **Removing a pillar is not innovation.** Dropping NPCs removed dialogue, choice, consequence and
  companionship, which are the reasons people play this series. Adding them back was the turning point.
- **Listen to the people who have built the thing before.** The reported pattern was known risks raised by
  specialists and QA and overruled.
- **Transmedia brings people in, the game decides whether they stay.** Both TV spikes decayed to the same
  baseline in under half a year and lowered the review average on the way through.
- **Community is an asset you can lose or earn.** The welcoming player culture did real reputational work
  in 2020 that marketing could not.

## Design Takeaways

- Do not ship on a fixed date into an obviously unready state. If a date is immovable, cut scope until what
  remains is stable. A long, honest public beta is cheaper than a decade of bad first impressions.
- Choose technology for the game being made. If our engine assumes single-player, either stay
  single-player (plus limited co-op) or budget years, not months, for the conversion.
- Keep people in the world. Whatever our multiplayer ambitions, quests need characters who talk back, want
  things and remember what the player did. Logs and terminals are seasoning, not the meal.
- If we have a shared world, decide early how choices persist: per-player instancing of story spaces
  (which *Wastelanders* used well) or genuinely server-wide state. Do not promise consequence and deliver
  resets.
- Make the map the star. The one thing nobody attacked was the place itself: distinct regions, readable
  landmarks, local folklore, stories told by set dressing. This is transferable and safe to learn from.
- Player housing that doubles as a shop and a showpiece creates reasons to visit strangers. Worth adopting
  in some form, with generous build budgets from day one.
- Drop-in public events with no lobby are low-friction social glue. Good model for optional co-op.
- Never sell the cure for friction we designed. Storage caps, fast-travel costs and repair sinks must be
  tuned for the non-paying player first. If there is a paid tier, it sells additions, not relief.
- Hold any cash shop to the stated rules ("cosmetic only" must stay cosmetic only). No fake discounts.
- Physical merchandise and partner products carry the game's name. Approve the actual shipped item, not
  the render. One nylon bag cost more goodwill than any single bug.
- When compensating for a mistake, over-correct on the first attempt. A token offer followed by a forced
  climb-down costs twice.
- Have a refund policy that matches consumer law in every market and state it plainly. Selling only
  through our own launcher removes the safety valve of a platform refund policy.
- Run a public test server from the start and keep regression suites for player-facing fixes; reintroduced
  bugs are read as incompetence.
- Protect QA: direct lines between testers and fixers, no mandatory crunch, and treat "QA already knew"
  as a release-blocking signal.
- Plan the long tail honestly. Free, regular, substantial updates did rebuild a playable game and a
  community. Plan the budget for that only if the launch is good enough to deserve it.
- Expect any future adaptation or publicity spike to deliver curious newcomers for about four months.
  Have an onboarding path ready before the spike, not after.
