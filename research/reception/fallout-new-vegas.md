# Reception: Fallout: New Vegas (2010)

Scope: critical and player reception of Obsidian's *Fallout: New Vegas* from launch (19 October 2010) to
November 2026, including aggregates, the Metacritic bonus story, DLC reception, retrospectives, concessions
its fans make, modding/replay culture, and sales.

Research note: several major outlets (IGN, Eurogamer, GameSpot, Giant Bomb, Polygon, The Guardian, Ars
Technica, Rock Paper Shotgun, NMA, Reddit) blocked direct fetching in this session, and the web-search budget
ran out part-way through. Where a claim rests on a secondary summary (Wikipedia's reception section,
Metacritic blurbs, search-result digests) or on the researcher's memory rather than the primary page, it is
flagged `[UNVERIFIED]` or attributed to the intermediary. See "Gaps" at the end.

## Quick Reference

- Launch Metascores: **84 (Xbox 360), 84 (PC), 82 (PS3)**. Good, but below *Fallout 3* (low 90s
  `[UNVERIFIED from memory]`), and one point short of a contractual bonus.
- Launch consensus: better writing, quests, factions and role-playing than *Fallout 3*; looks and feels like
  an expansion; **bugs, crashes, corrupted saves and frame-rate problems dominated the negative half of every
  review**.
- Score spread was unusually wide for a AAA sequel: The Guardian 5/5 and OXM US 9.5 at the top, Edge 6/10
  and 1Up "B" at the bottom, and the gap is almost entirely a disagreement about how much to dock for bugs.
- Edge's verdict is the cleanest statement of the launch view: creatively nearly everything right,
  mechanically and technically a "tragedy" (via Wikipedia's summary of the Edge review).
- Obsidian had roughly **18 months**, on an engine (Gamebryo) nobody at the studio had shipped on before.
- Contract: flat fee, no royalties, bonus only at **Metacritic 85+**. It scored 84. Chris Avellone disclosed
  this by tweet in March 2012, the same week Obsidian laid off about 30 people.
- Sales: Bethesda announced **5 million units and $300M revenue by 8 November 2010** (three weeks after
  launch); EEDAR estimated **11.6 million sold by 2015**.
- User aggregates today are far above critic aggregates: Metacritic user score **8.8 PC / 8.7 X360 / 8.2 PS3**;
  Steam roughly **95% positive across ~227,000 reviews**.
- Josh Sawyer (director) says the reputation took "about 5 years" to turn, and that launch reception "was
  not fantastic". The "always beloved" memory is false.
- Why it rose: patches plus community fixes removed the launch objection; *Fallout 4* (2015) and *Fallout 76*
  (2018) made its role-playing depth look scarce; long-form video essays (hbomberguy's has ~14M views)
  canonised the argument.
- DLC Metascores (X360): Dead Money 70, Honest Hearts 64, **Old World Blues 83**, Lonesome Road 63. Fan
  ranking has since diverged from critics, mostly upward for Dead Money and Lonesome Road.
- What fans concede: an empty, loading-screen-partitioned Strip; invisible walls; an underbuilt Legion
  (planned territory east of the Colorado was cut); no post-ending play; engine instability that still
  requires a mod guide on PC.
- Replay culture is the long tail: Steam all-time concurrent peak of **44,890 came in April 2024**, more than
  thirteen years after release, driven by the TV show; a second spike of **27,672 in February 2026** followed
  TV season 2 (set in New Vegas).
- January 2026 was the single busiest month for Steam reviews in the game's history (about 8,800 positive,
  270 negative).
- Standard advice in 2026 is still "play on PC with the Viva New Vegas mod guide" (Kotaku, January 2026).
- A yearly fan gathering has been held in the real Goodsprings, Nevada since 2022.
- Wikipedia (as read November 2026) states Bethesda announced remasters of *New Vegas* and *Fallout 3* in
  2026 `[UNVERIFIED beyond that one source]`.

Patch history (from Wikipedia's citations to IGN news): a "comprehensive" patch was announced on
8 November 2010 and a major update shipped around 10-11 December 2010. Reviews had already been filed
against the day-one build. Jason Schreier made exactly this point in 2012: many reviews docked the game for
bugs that were then patched, so the Metascore went stale almost immediately.

## The 84 vs. 85 Metacritic Bonus

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| Disclosure | 14-15 March 2012: Chris Avellone tweeted that the game was "a straight payment, no royalties", with a bonus only at 85+ on Metacritic, "which we didn't" get. |
| Scores | 84 (X360), 84 (PC), 82 (PS3). The contract reportedly counted if any version reached 85 (Wikipedia). |
| Context | Obsidian had just laid off about 30 staff after a next-gen Xbox project was cancelled (Kotaku). |
| Commercial contrast | The game had shipped 5M units / $300M within a month, so the publisher did very well regardless. |
| Bethesda | Declined to comment at the time. |
| Obsidian leadership | Feargus Urquhart later said it was simply what the contract said and that he was not upset (via Wikipedia). |
| Press reaction | Schreier (Kotaku), Kyle Orland (Ars Technica) and others argued Metacritic is opinion aggregation with undisclosed weighting and is unfit as a pay trigger. |
| Common error | Many retellings say Obsidian "lost royalties". `[DISPUTED]` Avellone's own wording says there were no royalties at all, only the bonus. Engadget's write-up used the royalties framing; Game Developer and Kotaku used the bonus framing. |
| Later clarification | A search digest reports that Avellone posted in 2021 saying Bethesda did not "screw over" Obsidian. `[UNVERIFIED]` Original post not located. |
| Fan afterlife | The "84" became a fandom in-joke: hbomberguy ends his essay by calling it the best game ever and awarding it 84/100; a change.org petition asks Metacritic to change the score to 85. |

Why it matters for reception history: the story reframed the launch bugs. Instead of "Obsidian shipped a
broken game", the popular narrative became "a great studio was given 18 months and then punished by a
number", which generated lasting sympathy and is part of why the game's reputation climbed.

### Timeline

|---|---|---|

### Why it rose (the causal story)

1. **The main launch complaint was fixable, and got fixed.** Bugs were the reason for a mid-80s score
   instead of a low-90s one. Patches, then community patches, removed most of that, while the things critics
   praised (writing, quests, factions) do not decay.
2. **Its strengths became scarce.** Skill-gated dialogue, killable quest-givers, mutually exclusive faction
   endings and failure states that the story absorbs were normal in 1998, unusual in 2010 and rare in AAA by
   2015-2018. Each later Bethesda entry made New Vegas look more distinctive.
3. **Replays reveal more than first plays.** The reputation system, the four-way ending and the per-town
   ending slides only show their range on a second or third character. Reviewers on deadline saw one path;
   fans saw four.
4. **The underdog narrative.** 18 months, borrowed engine, missed bonus by a point, layoffs.
5. **Mods kept it playable and fresh on PC** (see "Modding and Replay Culture").
6. **Creators with large audiences made the argument at length** (next section).
7. **Lineage.** Many Obsidian staff came from Black Isle; the game reuses ideas from the cancelled
   *Van Buren*. Fans of the first two games treated it as the "real" third entry. PC Gamer's retrospective
   reportedly called it the most authentic 3D *Fallout* (via GameBanshee's summary `[UNVERIFIED]`).

### Developer testimony against rose-tinted memory

- Josh Sawyer: it took "about 5 years" for players to come around, and longer for the team to believe the
  design choices had actually landed (PC Gamer / GamesRadar+ headlines; article bodies could not be loaded).
- Sawyer, answering fans who contrasted it with *Cyberpunk 2077*'s launch: the game was "ripped apart" and
  "its reputation at launch was not fantastic" (GamesRadar+ headline).
- A 2026 iXBT digest reports Sawyer attributing the rise partly to players forgetting their original
  complaints about reused assets and sameness `[UNVERIFIED, secondary]`.

## Core Arguments of the Major Retrospectives

### hbomberguy, "Fallout: New Vegas Is Genius, And Here's Why" (December 2020)

Metadata: published 19 or 20 December 2020 (sources differ by a day, likely time zone); ~14.3M views per a
third-party index in 2026. Length is `[DISPUTED]`: one index says about 1 h 37 min, another shows chapter
marks past three hours. Summary below is from a third-party transcript digest, not a first-hand viewing.

| Argument | Supporting examples used |
|---|---|
| Quests are layered so that one location pays off many threads | Vault 22 can be reached through about six different quests, has unique enemies, a moral choice about destroying or keeping research, and a hidden reward for thoroughness. |
| The opening is fast and plants questions | Shot in the head, dug up, out the door in minutes; compare *Fallout 3*'s long childhood tutorial. |
| Skills matter in conversation, and checks are transparent | Medicine, Science, Barter, Explosives etc. unlock lines; thresholds are deterministic and shown, including deliberately bad options when you lack the skill. |
| Traits and perks give horizontal, role-defining choices | Returning traits with a benefit and a cost; a tone toggle (Wild Wasteland); perks paced every other level so each matters. |
| It absorbed what modders had added to *Fallout 3* | Iron sights, weapon mods, survival mode: "build in what players would otherwise mod in". |
| Fixed-difficulty zones create real danger and a sense of growth | Lethal creatures north of the start town teach geography through failure; contrast with level scaling. |
| Landmarks and overlapping quests solve content discovery | The casino tower visible from the start, the roadside dinosaur, the solar plant. Multiple quests point at the same places. |
| The player is trusted with control | Almost anyone can be killed and the story routes around it through notes, replacements and alternative quest-givers. |
| Moral choices lack a "correct" answer | Water theft in a poor district; where to send a power plant's output; whether isolationists deserve help. |
| Factions are arguments, not teams | The republic is the obvious choice but overstretched and corrupt; the slaver empire is monstrous but coherent; the autocrat and the independent path each have real costs. |
| The ending cashes in earlier choices | Who shows up to the final battle depends on what you did; the final boss can be talked down by several different skills. |
| Conceded flaws | Engine crashes, memory problems, trouble on modern Windows and high-refresh displays; final battle is a "steamroll" if you recruited everyone. |

### Noah Caldwell-Gervais, "A Thorough Look at Fallout" (series retrospective) `[UNVERIFIED, from memory; not consulted this session]`

- Treats the series chronologically and reads New Vegas as a western about what kind of society should be
  rebuilt, where the earlier Bethesda entry was about surviving a ruin.
- Emphasises that the Mojave is organised around roads, supply lines and territorial claims, so geography
  itself expresses the political conflict.
- Values the DLC as a connected four-part meditation on letting go of the old world, and treats the game as
  the point where the series' themes are most coherent.
- Concedes the same technical and structural limits others do.

### Many A True Nerd (Jon) `[UNVERIFIED, from memory; not consulted this session]`

- Best known in this debate for a long rebuttal defending *Fallout 3* against hbomberguy's earlier
  "Fallout 3 Is Garbage" video, and for exhaustive New Vegas challenge playthroughs (permadeath, kill
  everything, no-kill).
- His contribution to New Vegas's reputation is less a single thesis than demonstration: challenge runs
  that show the game holds together under extreme self-imposed rules, because nearly every quest tolerates
  dead NPCs, pacifism or missing skills.
- Useful counterweight: he argues the New Vegas-versus-3 framing undervalues exploration-first design.

## Lessons: Launch Quality, Trust, Long-Tail Reputation

- **A review score is a snapshot of the worst build.** New Vegas was scored on its day-one state, then
  patched. The number never moved; the game did. If reputation or pay is tied to launch reviews, launch
  stability is worth more than almost any feature.
- **Fixable flaws fade, structural strengths compound.** Bugs can be patched by you or your community.
  Reactivity, writing and faction design cannot be patched in later, and they are what people are still
  discussing sixteen years on.
- **A game can lose the launch and win the decade,** but only if the underlying design rewards repeat play
  and the platform lets players repair and extend it.
- **Sympathy is reputational capital.** The bonus story turned a "buggy game" into a "wronged studio".
  Transparency about constraints, even after the fact, changes how flaws are read.
- **Scarcity inflates value.** New Vegas's standing rose every time a successor offered less role-playing.
  Being the only modern game that does a thing well is a durable position.
- **Cuts leave scars exactly where the pitch pointed.** The game is named after a city whose centrepiece is
  its weakest space, and it is a two-sided war with one side barely built. Players forgive, but they notice.

## Design Takeaways

- Budget for stability before content. Obsidian's own late call (optimise instead of adding post-game
  content) was right, and still not enough. Lock scope early enough that the last months are bug-fixing.
- Test the showpiece location on the weakest target hardware first, not last. If the hub will not run,
  everything else in the pitch is compromised.
- Make the central conflict's sides equally playable. If we have two great powers, each needs territory the
  player can walk through, civilians, and quest lines of comparable length. Cut a side faction before
  starving a main one.
- Build quests that survive the player: any named character can die, and the plot must still resolve.
  Design this in as a rule from the first quest, not as a late feature.
- Show skill thresholds in dialogue and make checks deterministic. Transparency was repeatedly singled out
  as respectful of the player.
- Let several quests converge on each major location. It solves discovery and makes the world feel
  interlinked at low cost.
- Use fixed-danger regions and visible landmarks instead of level scaling to give geography meaning, but
  avoid invisible walls. If an area is off-limits, use terrain, hazards or enemies the player can read.
- Ship an optional hardcore/survival layer as a toggle. It was praised by exactly the players who wanted
  it and ignored by the rest.
- End-state slides per community are cheap and are one of the most cited reasons for replays. Plan them.
  If possible also allow continued play after the ending, since its absence is still a standing complaint.
- Plan DLC as a serial with cross-references if we do DLC at all; players reward the connected reading.
  But each part must also stand alone mechanically, because critics will score it that way.
- Make the game moddable and keep it purchasable and runnable on new hardware. The long tail here is
  community-maintained. Consider shipping an official stability branch years later instead of leaving it to
  a fan guide.
- Never tie team compensation to a third-party aggregate. If milestones must exist, use ones the team
  controls (crash rate, completion rate, refund rate).
- Do not count on a redemption arc. Sawyer's five-year figure is a warning: most studios do not survive
  five years of waiting for the audience to come around.
